# Delivery Platform Category-Level Data: Seafood/Lobster vs. Dessert/Waffles/Crepes (US, UberEats/DoorDash/Grubhub proxy research)

## What do UberEats, DoorDash, and Grubhub's own published trend reports say about growth in seafood category orders vs. dessert/sweet-treat category orders in the US?

### Takeaway
None of the three platforms' own published consumer trend reports (DoorDash's annual delivery/restaurant trends reports, Uber Eats' "Cravings Report") break out seafood vs. dessert category order growth in a comparable, quantified way for the US market. DoorDash's reports mention desserts/sweets qualitatively (as a "guilty pleasure" behavior) but give no seafood-specific growth figure; seafood category growth data that does exist comes from third-party foodservice-industry research (Circana, Datassential), not from the delivery platforms themselves, and is about restaurant traffic/menu penetration generally, not delivery-specific order volume.

### Cited Findings
- DoorDash's trend reporting states that when asked about "guilty pleasure" delivery orders, Gen Z and Millennials lean toward fast food, Gen X stay loyal to pizza, and Boomers go "straight to dessert with cake and ice cream" — a demographic/occasion framing, not a category growth metric — [DoorDash Delivery Trends Report coverage via search summary](https://about.doordash.com/en-us/news/doordash-delivery-trends-report)
- DoorDash's 2026 Restaurant Industry Trends Report ("From the Doorstep to the Dining Room") covers the "full restaurant guest journey" but no seafood- or dessert-specific order-growth figures surfaced in available coverage — [DoorDash newsroom](https://about.doordash.com/en-us/news/doordash-restaurant-industry-trends-report-2026)
- Uber Eats' 2025 Cravings Report (as covered in regional press, e.g. South Africa, Australia, New Zealand) highlights trending items such as fried chicken, butter chicken/naan combos, matcha drinks (+217% over two years in NSW, Australia), and malva pudding as a top dessert in South Africa — but these are international market editions with no comparable US seafood-vs-dessert breakout found — [Uber Eats 2025 Cravings Report (NZ)](https://www.uber.com/nz/en/newsroom/uber-eats-lifts-the-lid-on-kiwi-cravings-30000-butter-chickens-and-51-bananas-later/); [Australia 1-billion-deliveries release](https://www.uber.com/au/en/newsroom/uber-eats-hits-one-billion-deliveries-and-reveals-australias-biggest-cravings-of-2025/); [South Africa coverage](https://www.ewn.co.za/2025/12/15/uber-eats-cravings-report-reveals-what-south-africans-ordered-most-in-2025)
- DoorDash's "America's Favorite Delivery Spots" cuisine-based feature reported African cuisine orders up 61% year-over-year — illustrating DoorDash does sometimes publish cuisine-level YoY growth figures, but not for a seafood-vs-dessert comparison — [DoorDash newsroom](https://about.doordash.com/en-us/news/americas-favorite-delivery-spots-on-doordash-2025)
- Independent of delivery platforms: Circana reported that traffic at seafood restaurants fell 1% in 2024 while sales were flat for the year — [SeafoodSource, citing Circana/NRN](https://www.seafoodsource.com/news/foodservice-retail/seafood-sees-drops-in-menu-penetration-at-us-restaurants-in-2024)
- Datassential data: fish and shellfish appeared on roughly 75% and 68% of US restaurant menus respectively in 2024, down 4% and 3% from 2020; tuna penetration fell 11%, crab and scallop each fell 14% — [SeafoodSource/Datassential](https://www.seafoodsource.com/news/foodservice-retail/seafood-sees-drops-in-menu-penetration-at-us-restaurants-in-2024)
- Contradicting the menu-penetration decline, the same reporting states US seafood consumption rose 20% in 2024 across all age groups, with the largest gains among Gen Z — this is a notable internal contradiction in the secondary source (declining menu presence vs. rising consumption) that should be flagged, not resolved — [SeafoodSource](https://www.seafoodsource.com/news/foodservice-retail/seafood-sees-drops-in-menu-penetration-at-us-restaurants-in-2024)
- A seafood-industry trend piece (NRN) frames seafood as having "growth opportunity on menus," implying current under-penetration rather than current strength — [NRN](https://www.nrn.com/food-trends/trend-expert-seafood-has-growth-opportunity-on-menus)
- Generic market-research-mill reports (not primary, lower confidence) claim a "Global Seafood Takeout Market" valued at $36.45B in 2025 growing at 6.2% CAGR, and that "44% of seafood restaurants have expanded delivery-focused operations" and "64% of consumers prefer online seafood ordering" — these figures come from SEO-style market-report aggregators (GlobalGrowthInsights-type sites) with no visible primary methodology, and should be treated as low-confidence/unverifiable rather than platform-sourced data — [GlobalGrowthInsights seafood takeout market](https://www.globalgrowthinsights.com/market-reports/seafood-takeout-market-126366)

### Inferences
- The near-total absence of seafood-vs-dessert category comparisons in platform-published materials itself suggests neither category is treated by the platforms as a headline "trend story" category the way items like chicken, matcha, or specific cuisines are — platform marketing content skews toward cuisine (ethnic food types), beverages, and viral/novelty items rather than protein/dessert category-pair comparisons.
- The seafood industry's own trajectory (declining restaurant menu penetration 2020–2024, flat-to-declining traffic in 2024, per Circana/Datassential) is a headwind for a seafood-forward delivery concept's addressable market, independent of delivery-specific execution — even though underlying consumption may be rising through retail/grocery channels rather than restaurants.

### Gaps
- No platform (DoorDash, Uber Eats, Grubhub) publishes a direct, quantified US seafood-category-order-growth-rate vs. dessert-category-order-growth-rate comparison in any report found. This is a genuine data gap, not an oversight in search — Grubhub/Just Eat Takeaway in particular publishes very little consumer trend content compared to DoorDash and Uber Eats.
- No confirmation of what year-over-year order growth (platform-reported) looks like specifically for "seafood" or "dessert/bakery" as a stand-alone cuisine/category tag inside any platform's own US cuisine-trend list (DoorDash's cuisine spotlight pieces name cuisines like African, not food-type categories like seafood or dessert).

## Is there published data on order frequency / repeat-purchase rate by food category — i.e., is seafood/lobster "low frequency, high ticket" vs. dessert/waffle "high frequency, low ticket"?

### Takeaway
No platform publishes category-level order frequency or repeat-purchase-rate data (seafood vs. dessert or otherwise) in any public report found; the only frequency metrics platforms disclose are aggregate, company-wide (not category-split) figures such as DoorDash's "average order frequency" for its New Verticals (grocery/retail/convenience) cohort. The "high frequency/low ticket vs. low frequency/high ticket" framing has to be treated as a plausible-but-unverified inference from general food-industry logic (desserts/snacks are classic impulse, add-on, high-frequency, low-ticket items; seafood/lobster are classic occasion-driven, high-ticket, lower-frequency items), not as a sourced platform finding.

### Cited Findings
- DoorDash disclosed that for its US "New Verticals" segment (grocery, alcohol, retail — not a food-type breakdown), consumer engagement grew in Q3 2025 with increases in monthly active users, "average order frequency," and average order values, driving accelerated YoY gross order volume growth — this is a cross-vertical, not cuisine/category-specific, frequency metric — [FreightWaves / DoorDash Q3 2025 coverage](https://www.freightwaves.com/news/a-tale-of-2-food-apps-uber-eats-grows-doordash-finds-new-verticals)
- DoorDash disclosed that the share of its US monthly active users ordering from at least one New Verticals category rose from 20% in December 2023 to 25% in December 2024 — again a verticals-level (grocery/retail), not food-category-level, metric — [Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/operations/delivery-apps-like-uber-and-doordash-are-seeing-growth-in-new-verticals/)
- General (non-platform-specific) industry stat: 37% of US adults order restaurant delivery at least once a week, and Americans average 4.6 orders per month through third-party delivery apps — an aggregate frequency figure with no category split — [DoorDash merchant blog compilation, as surfaced via secondary aggregation](https://merchants.doordash.com/en-us/blog/food-delivery-statistics) (note: this DoorDash merchants.doordash.com page itself could not be directly fetched/verified in this research session — network egress to doordash.com domains was blocked — so this figure is reported here as a search-engine-summarized claim attributed to that page, not independently confirmed from the primary document)
- A restaurant-operations industry source (CloudKitchens) frames order frequency broadly as "one of the most important indicators of retention strength," noting a weekly-ordering customer creates more long-term value than several one-time discount-driven buyers — a general retention-economics point, not category-specific data — [CloudKitchens blog](https://cloudkitchens.com/blog/delivery-customer-retention)

### Inferences
- Because platforms disclose frequency only at the aggregate or broad-vertical level (prepared food vs. grocery vs. alcohol/retail), and never at the cuisine or food-type level, any claim that "seafood is low-frequency/high-ticket" or "desserts are high-frequency/low-ticket" on delivery platforms specifically is an inference from general restaurant-industry/menu-engineering logic (desserts/snacks are classically impulse/add-on items ordered more often at lower price points; seafood/lobster are classically special-occasion, higher-ticket items ordered less often) — it is not something DoorDash, Uber Eats, or Grubhub has published data to confirm or deny for delivery specifically.
- The fact that no platform has ever published category-level frequency/retention data (despite publishing plenty of other granular stats — cuisine YoY growth, item-level "most ordered" lists, demographic breakdowns) suggests this data either isn't something they choose to make public, or isn't something that maps cleanly onto their internal taxonomy in a way that produces a clean marketing statistic. Either way, the absence itself is informative: it's not just that this researcher failed to find it.

### Gaps
- No public, platform-sourced figure exists for "average days between orders" or "% repeat customers within 30/60/90 days" segmented by food category (seafood, dessert, or any other) on DoorDash, Uber Eats, or Grubhub. This is the central gap relative to the internal Glovo proprietary dataset the user is trying to proxy, and it should be reported to the user as a hard limit of public data, not inferred around.
- No public data found on average order value (ticket size) by category either, which would be needed to actually test the "high-frequency/low-ticket vs. low-frequency/high-ticket" hypothesis with real numbers rather than logic.

## Is there data on how temperature/texture-sensitive categories (ice cream/desserts needing to stay cold vs. fried/hot items) perform on ratings/complaints, as a proxy for delivery execution risk by category?

### Takeaway
There is no platform-published, systematic ratings/complaint-rate data broken out by food category or temperature-sensitivity; what exists is anecdotal consumer-review and food-journalism commentary (not rigorous data) converging on the same qualitative point: both fried/breaded hot items (fries, fried chicken, fried seafood/calamari) and cold items requiring temperature control (ice cream) are flagged repeatedly as high-complaint/high-risk delivery categories, for different underlying mechanisms (sogginess from trapped steam vs. melting from transit time).

### Cited Findings
- Multiple consumer-food-media roundups (Cheapism, HuffPost, AOL, Top10.com, Timeout) consistently list fried and breaded foods — specifically including fried seafood and calamari — among the worst-performing items for delivery, citing steam buildup in containers causing sogginess; french fries, fried chicken, and ramen noodles are named in the same category of texture failure — [AOL "Worst Foods to Order for Delivery"](https://www.aol.com/articles/worst-foods-order-delivery-ones-223000661.html); [Top10.com](https://www.top10.com/food-delivery/top-10-foods-you-should-never-order-for-delivery)
- The same body of consumer-facing coverage explicitly names "fried seafood," and calamari specifically, as faring worst on taste/texture after delivery — [search-summarized coverage of AOL/food-media roundups](https://www.aol.com/articles/15-delivery-foods-always-show-164500000.html)
- Anecdotal customer reviews (ReviewsIO aggregation of DoorDash reviews) include specific complaints of ice cream/frozen items arriving melted, with one customer reporting ice cream fully melted and receiving only a $2 credit on a $30 order; DoorDash's aggregate rating on this review platform was 1.3/5 with only 7% of reviewers recommending the service — note this is a self-selected negative-review aggregator (ReviewsIO), not a platform-disclosed complaint rate, and is subject to strong selection bias toward dissatisfied customers — [ReviewsIO](https://www.reviews.io/company-reviews/store/doordash)
- By contrast, individual Uber Eats ice cream/creperie merchant listings show high per-store ratings (e.g., one Cold Stone Creamery location at 4.7/5 across 900+ ratings, with a customer comment "despite my worries of melting" it arrived "just like going into the store"), while another listing shows a complaint of "1/2 melted before delivery" — illustrating wide store-level variance rather than a consistent category-wide failure rate — [Uber Eats store listings, search-summarized](https://www.ubereats.com/store/cold-stone-creamery-896-s-29th-st-w/BmFKD3FsWSui0XfdA7j98Q)
- Stewy/sauced items (braises, soups) and sauce-coated items are repeatedly named by food writers as the best-performing delivery categories, reheating or holding up well because they're designed to sit in liquid — relevant as the structural contrast case to both seafood (often fried/delicate) and desserts (often temperature-critical) — [HuffPost "Best And Worst Foods To Have Delivered"](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/best-worst-foods-for-delivery_l_64bab4e2e4b08a8c92107cd7)

### Inferences
- Both target categories in the underlying comparison carry delivery-execution risk, but of different kinds: a seafood/lobster concept's risk is concentrated in fried/breaded preparations and in delicate proteins overcooking or drying out in transit (per the recurring "fried seafood/calamari" callouts), whereas a dessert/waffle/crepe concept's risk is concentrated in temperature-sensitive components (ice cream, whipped cream, anything meant to be eaten frozen or cold) rather than in the base product (a plain waffle or crepe batter travels more like bread than like fried seafood). This suggests a lobster roll or lobster-based dish not requiring deep-frying, and a waffle/crepe not paired with ice cream, would each individually mitigate their category's worst-case failure mode — but no platform data confirms actual comparative failure/refund rates between the two categories.
- The wide store-level rating variance seen in the Uber Eats examples (4.7 vs. complaints at other locations for the same item type, ice cream) suggests operational execution (packaging, distance/time to delivery, insulation) may matter more than category per se — consistent with Alberto/Glovo's reported finding that category (not brand) drives baseline metrics, but qualified by the finding that within a category, operational choices still swing outcomes substantially.

### Gaps
- No platform (DoorDash, Uber Eats, Grubhub) publishes an aggregate complaint rate, refund rate, or star-rating distribution by food category. All category-level quality-perception data found here is anecdotal (individual reviews, food-journalism roundups) rather than statistically representative — this should be reported to the user as "directionally consistent anecdotal signal, not data," per the research instructions to flag speculation vs. confirmed fact.
- No source quantifies how much more often seafood orders vs. dessert orders are refunded, re-delivered, or rated poorly specifically due to temperature/texture failure.

## Published commission rate structures, delivery-radius norms, or category-specific promotional support that could differentially help/hurt a seafood concept vs. a dessert/crepe concept

### Takeaway
Published commission-fee structures on DoorDash and Uber Eats are tiered by service-plan level (Basic/Plus/Premier or Lite/Plus/Premium), not by food category — so there is no publicly disclosed commission advantage or penalty for seafood vs. dessert concepts specifically. The platforms do run cuisine- and occasion-based promotional/discovery features (homepage carousels, "trending cuisine" spotlights, "America's Favorite Delivery Spots by Cuisine"), but none of the coverage found names seafood or dessert specifically as a promotionally favored or disfavored category; promotion appears to go to cuisines (e.g., African cuisine, up 61% YoY per DoorDash) and to items that are already virally trending (matcha, specific regional dishes), not to broad food-type categories like "seafood" or "dessert."

### Cited Findings
- DoorDash's commission tiers are Basic (15%), Plus (25%), and Premier (30%), with a 6% rate for US pickup orders meeting price-match terms — flat across all restaurant/food types, no category-specific rate disclosed — [Zay-OS compilation of DoorDash fee structure](https://zay-os.com/restaurant-delivery-commission-statistics)
- Uber Eats' commission tiers are Lite (20%), Plus (25%, rising to 30% for Uber One member orders), and Premium (30%), with 7% pickup fee for validated in-store pricing (10% otherwise); the Lite tier rose from 15% to 20% and pickup fees from 6% to 7% as of a March rate change — again flat across food categories — [Food On Demand coverage of Uber Eats fee changes](https://foodondemand.com/04012026/how-the-uber-eats-fees-stack-up-against-3pd-competitors/)
- When accounting for hidden costs (DashPass/Uber One subsidies, marketing charges, error adjustments), effective commission can reach 28–35% of gross revenue on DoorDash and 29–36% on Uber Eats — again not category-differentiated — [Food On Demand / industry fee analysis](https://foodondemand.com/04012026/how-the-uber-eats-fees-stack-up-against-3pd-competitors/)
- DoorDash uses homepage/category-page "Spotlight" display and video ad formats, and curates cuisine-based "trending" lists (e.g., its "America's Favorite Delivery Spots" by cuisine feature, which reported African cuisine orders up 61% YoY) — these are paid or curated promotional slots organized by cuisine/occasion, not by food-type category like seafood or dessert specifically — [DoorDash newsroom](https://about.doordash.com/en-us/news/americas-favorite-delivery-spots-on-doordash-2025); [Osmos ad-format overview](https://www.osmos.ai/blog/food-delivery-retail-media-monetization-2026)
- Uber Eats offers a "Top Eats" badge/ranking feature based on reviews/ratings/performance, and a swipeable merchant carousel ad format, plus a user-generated "featured lists" discovery carousel — again cuisine/merchant-level, not food-category-level, promotional mechanisms — [JigsawKraft ranking guide](https://www.jigsawkraft.com/post/how-to-rank-higher-on-doordash-uber-eats-restaurant-optimization-guide-2026); [Food On Demand](https://foodondemand.com/10232024/uber-eats-now-lets-users-create-and-share-lists-of-favorite-spots-on-app/)

### Inferences
- Because commission structures are plan-tier-based (volume/service-level) rather than category-based, a seafood concept and a dessert/crepe concept face identical headline commission economics on DoorDash and Uber Eats; any difference in unit economics between the two concepts would have to come from differences in average ticket size, ingredient/packaging cost structure, or order frequency — not from the platforms' published fee schedules.
- Promotional "trending" placement on both platforms appears to reward virality and cuisine identity (ethnic cuisines, specific trending items like matcha) more than broad food-type categories — meaning neither a generic "seafood" nor a generic "dessert" positioning is obviously better-supported by current platform promotional mechanics; a narrower, more "ownable" positioning (e.g., a distinct cuisine or viral-item framing) may matter more than the broad category choice itself for earning algorithmic/promotional visibility.

### Gaps
- No public data found on delivery-radius norms set differently by food category (e.g., whether platforms cap delivery radius more tightly for temperature-sensitive or fragile items like ice cream or raw/delicate seafood). This may exist in merchant-facing operational settings but was not found in public reporting.
- No evidence found either way on whether platforms give algorithmic/search-ranking boosts to specific food-type categories (as opposed to cuisines or individual trending items) — this remains an open question.
- No Grubhub-specific commission or promotional data was found in this round of research (its US market share, per one secondary source, is around 16%, behind DoorDash ~56% and Uber Eats ~23%, per unverified aggregator figures); Grubhub-specific published trend/promotional content appears to be considerably thinner than DoorDash's or Uber Eats'.

## Data on how "fresh/premium ingredient" categories (like good seafood) perform on ghost-kitchen/virtual-brand strategies, versus commodity dessert items

### Takeaway
There is no platform-published, quantified performance comparison between premium/fresh-ingredient virtual brands (seafood) and commodity dessert virtual brands; however, industry commentary and observed market structure both point toward desserts/sweets being the more common, more operationally forgiving virtual-brand/ghost-kitchen category (low prep complexity, shelf-stable or freezer-stable inputs, easy multi-brand kitchen-sharing), while seafood is explicitly flagged by food-delivery commentary as one of the categories that "doesn't travel well," making it a structurally harder fit for a delivery-only or ghost-kitchen virtual-brand model.

### Cited Findings
- Ghost Kitchen Brands (now Ghost Kitchens International) built a multi-brand virtual-restaurant model that explicitly includes dessert brands (Cinnabon, Ben & Jerry's) operating alongside other quick-service brands (Nathan's, Wow Bao, Jamba, Donatos) out of shared kitchens, and expanded into physical retail hosting (30 US Walmart locations) — demonstrating commodity/known-brand dessert concepts are a proven, scaled component of the virtual-brand model — [SupermarketNews](https://www.supermarketnews.com/foodservice-retail/ghost-kitchen-brands-makes-its-walmart-debut); [Wikipedia: Virtual restaurant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_restaurant)
- Food-delivery consumer-advice coverage states plainly that "most seafood is listed among items that don't survive the delivery drive well, along with items like french fries, tempura, and cream-sauce pasta" — a direct structural argument against seafood as a delivery-first/virtual-brand category — [search-summarized consumer food-delivery coverage](https://www.webstaurantstore.com/blog/3792/virtual-restaurant-brands.html) (note: this specific framing appeared in the aggregated search summary rather than being independently re-verified by direct fetch of a single named article; treat as a directionally consistent but not deeply sourced claim)
- No named seafood-specific virtual brand (equivalent in scale/recognition to a Cinnabon-by-Ghost-Kitchens or a MrBeast Burger) surfaced in this research round — the absence of a well-known scaled seafood virtual brand, compared to multiple well-known dessert/snack virtual brands, is itself notable and consistent with the "doesn't travel well" structural concern — [search coverage of ghost kitchen brand rosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_restaurant)

### Inferences
- The virtual-brand/ghost-kitchen ecosystem's revealed preference — multiple scaled dessert-based virtual brands exist and are actively promoted by multi-brand ghost kitchen operators and by retailers (Walmart), while no comparably scaled seafood virtual brand surfaced — is a meaningful proxy data point for structural delivery-channel fit, even though it is inferred from absence/presence rather than from a direct platform statement. It suggests the ghost-kitchen/virtual-brand channel, specifically, structurally favors commodity dessert/snack concepts over fresh/premium seafood concepts, independent of consumer demand for seafood as a dine-in category.
- This is a different claim from "seafood has weak demand" — the industry data in the first section shows mixed-to-weakening seafood restaurant traffic/menu penetration but rising underlying consumption; the virtual-brand data here is specifically about delivery/ghost-kitchen channel fit, i.e., a seafood concept's challenge may be more about execution/format (can it be made to travel well, e.g., chilled lobster rolls vs. fried/hot seafood) than about underlying consumer appetite.

### Gaps
- No platform or industry source publishes direct sales, order-volume, or survival-rate data comparing seafood virtual brands to dessert virtual brands. This entire section rests on indirect/structural evidence (what concepts exist at scale, what food commentary says travels well) rather than on a cited performance statistic, and should be presented to the reader as inference, not confirmed fact.
- No data found on complaint/refund rates specifically for seafood vs. dessert virtual brands (as distinct from dine-in restaurants offering the same items via delivery).

## Overall note on source quality and data availability (cross-cutting)

- Primary platform sources (DoorDash newsroom at about.doordash.com, DoorDash merchant blog at merchants.doordash.com) could not be directly fetched in this research session — outbound requests to doordash.com subdomains were blocked by this environment's network egress proxy. All DoorDash-sourced claims above are therefore based on WebSearch result summaries of those pages (and of secondary coverage quoting them), not on independently re-verified primary-document text. This should be flagged to the reader as a methodological limitation of this research pass, and a human reviewer with unrestricted web access should ideally re-verify the DoorDash-attributed figures directly against about.doordash.com and merchants.doordash.com before they are treated as fully confirmed.
- Uber Eats primary newsroom pages (uber.com/newsroom, regional uber.com/xx/en/newsroom pages) were reachable in principle via search summaries but were likewise not independently re-fetched for full-text verification in this pass.
- Several numeric claims in wide circulation (seafood takeout market size/CAGR, "64% of consumers prefer online seafood ordering," "44% of seafood restaurants expanded delivery operations") come from SEO-style market-research-aggregator sites (e.g., GlobalGrowthInsights, MarketResearchFuture-style publishers) whose methodology is not visible or verifiable. These are flagged explicitly above as low-confidence and should not be treated as equivalent in reliability to Circana/Datassential-sourced trade-press figures (SeafoodSource, NRN) or to platform-disclosed investor metrics (DoorDash's New Verticals MAU/frequency stats from its actual earnings-related disclosures).
- The central, highest-confidence finding of this entire research task is a negative one: none of DoorDash, Uber Eats, or Grubhub publishes category-level (seafood vs. dessert, or any other food-type pairing) order-frequency, retention, or repeat-purchase data. Every frequency/retention metric these platforms disclose publicly is either company-wide or split by broad vertical (prepared food vs. grocery vs. alcohol/retail), never by cuisine or food type. Any attempt to map "seafood = low-frequency/high-ticket" and "dessert = high-frequency/low-ticket" onto real platform numbers is necessarily an inference from general restaurant-industry/menu-engineering logic and from indirect proxies (virtual-brand roster composition, food-journalism "what travels well" consensus, Circana/Datassential restaurant-traffic data), not a sourced platform statistic. This gap should be stated plainly in the final report rather than papered over.
