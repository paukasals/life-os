# Miami/South Florida Local Market Context for Lobsteria & TCAWBAR Expansion

Note: Lobsteria (144 NE 27th St, Wynwood, Miami, a 1977 Airstream, Maine lobster rolls/oysters/ceviche, open 5pm–2am) and The Crepes and Waffles Bar / TCAWBAR share the **same street address** (144 NE 27th St, Miami) per Yelp and Tripadvisor listings — i.e., the two concepts currently appear to operate out of the same Wynwood location/commissary, likely on shared or overlapping real estate, which is directly relevant to any "second location" analysis. [Lobsteria Yelp](https://www.yelp.com/biz/lobsteria-miami-2); [TCAWBAR Yelp](https://www.yelp.com/biz/the-crepes-and-waffles-bar-miami-3); [Lobsteria site](https://www.lobsteria.co/)

## How many direct competitors exist in Miami/South Florida for (a) a premium/artisanal lobster roll concept and (b) a dedicated crepes/waffles concept?

### Takeaway
Direct, named competitors are limited and mostly fall into two tiers: national lobster-roll truck/fast-casual franchises (Cousins Maine Lobster, Mason's Famous Lobster Rolls) priced below Lobsteria's premium positioning, and a handful of independent trucks/stalls (Mr. Lobster, The Lobsta Guy, Lobsta Rolls) of unclear quality/positioning; for crepes/waffles, competition is a small but growing set of sit-down creperies (Sweet Paris, Stephanie's Crepes) that are expanding aggressively in the Miami area, none of which are structured as a delivery-first "bar" concept like TCAWBAR.

### Cited Findings
- Named Miami-area lobster roll trucks/restaurants found via search: Mr. Lobster, Cousins Maine Lobster (Miami truck), The Lobsta Guy (3.9★, 27 reviews), Mason's Famous Lobster Rolls (Downtown Miami, 3.8★, 92 reviews), Lobsta Rolls (Calle Ocho/Little Havana truck) — [Roaming Hunger](https://roaminghunger.com/mr-lobster/); [Roaming Hunger — Cousins](https://roaminghunger.com/cousins-maine-lobster-miami/); [Yelp](https://www.yelp.com/search?find_desc=Lobster+Roll+Truck&find_loc=Miami,+FL); [Yelp — Lobsta Rolls](https://www.yelp.com/biz/lobsta-rolls-miami)

### Inferences
- The lobster-roll competitive set in Miami-Dade proper skews toward mid-priced national franchise trucks (Cousins Maine Lobster at ~$18–22/item) rather than Lobsteria's stated premium ceiling ($75+), implying Lobsteria may have limited direct price-tier competition at its top end, but faces more competition in its lower price band from Cousins/Mason's/Lobsta Rolls.

- Because Lobsteria and TCAWBAR already co-locate at one Wynwood address, a "second location" decision for either brand is effectively a decision about whether to replicate this same two-brand bundling model elsewhere or split them into separate footprints.

## What is Miami/South Florida's tourism volume and visitor demographic trend?

### Takeaway
Miami-Dade tourism hit a record 28.3 million visitors and $32.2 billion in economic impact in 2025, with visitor dining/lodging/shopping spend up 4.1% year-over-year — but the growth is now being driven by domestic (Florida-resident) visitation offsetting a meaningful decline in international arrivals, a trend with direct relevance to Lobsteria's "tourist + late-night Wynwood crowd" strategy.

### Cited Findings
- Miami-Dade County welcomed 28.3 million visitors in 2025, generating $32.2 billion in total economic impact — a record — [Hotel News Resource / GMCVB](https://www.hotelnewsresource.com/article141536.html); [PR Newswire / GMCVB](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/greater-miami-convention--visitors-bureau-reports-strong-tourism-performance-positioning-miami-dade-county-for-continued-growth-302791987.html)
- Visitors spent $22.7 billion on lodging, dining, shopping, transportation and entertainment in 2025, up 4.1% from 2024; lodging alone generated $10.4 billion, nearly half of all visitor expenditures — [GMCVB via Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/articles/greater-miami-convention-visitors-bureau-193400956.html)
- Tourism represented about 8% of Miami-Dade County's GDP in 2025, generated $5.3 billion+ in combined federal/state/local tax revenue, and supported 216,000+ jobs (10% of all Miami-Dade employment) with $12 billion+ in hospitality-sector wages — [GMCVB report](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/articles/greater-miami-convention-visitors-bureau-193400956.html)
- Top international visitor origin markets for Miami-Dade: Colombia, Brazil, United Kingdom, Canada, Mexico, Argentina, Spain, Italy, Germany, and France — [GMCVB](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/articles/greater-miami-convention-visitors-bureau-193400956.html)
- Despite the 2025 record overall, international visitation specifically declined: Colombia arrivals at MIA fell 12%, Dominican Republic fell 20%, and Canada fell 8%; total tourism was still up ~5% only because of increased visitation from other parts of Florida (domestic "drive market" visitors) offsetting the international drop — [WLRN](https://www.wlrn.org/business/2025-11-07/miami-dade-tourism)
- Contributing factors to the international decline cited by reporting: new U.S. federal registration/fingerprinting rules for foreign visitors staying 30+ days, Canadian tourists reducing visits in response to U.S. tariffs on Canadian goods, and a slowing global economy reducing discretionary international travel budgets — [WLRN](https://www.wlrn.org/business/2025-11-07/miami-dade-tourism)
- GMCVB projects 2026 total visitation to be roughly on par with 2025, with growth in Florida-resident visits expected to offset continued softness in international visitation — [Miami Today](https://www.miamitodaynews.com/?p=85910)
- First-half-2025 data showed an 8.2% year-over-year decline in international overnight arrivals to Florida broadly, part of a "nine consecutive months" of U.S. tourism decline affecting Florida alongside Texas, Kentucky, Michigan, Illinois and New Mexico — [AOL/Yahoo Finance](https://www.aol.com/finance/florida-restaurants-shutting-down-amid-203500415.html); [Travel and Tour World](https://www.travelandtourworld.com/news/article/florida-joins-texas-kentucky-michigan-illinois-new-mexico-and-others-in-facing-successive-decline-in-us-tourism-for-nine-consecutive-months-in-2025-everything-you-need-to-know/)
- Anecdotal restaurant-level impact: Flora Plant Kitchen (Miami's MiMo district, near Wynwood) saw a 40% sales drop in summer 2025 vs. spring, attributed to the traditional tourist season now ending by May instead of June, leaving a roughly six-month low-tourist gap before snowbirds/international visitors return in December — [AOL/Yahoo Finance](https://www.aol.com/finance/florida-restaurants-shutting-down-amid-203500415.html)

### Inferences
- A business model leaning heavily on international tourist foot traffic (as Lobsteria's Wynwood positioning partly does) is currently exposed to a real, multi-month 2025 headwind — international arrivals are down double digits in some key feeder markets (Colombia, Canada, Dominican Republic) even as total visitation hits a record on the back of Florida-resident trips.
- This implies the late-night Wynwood crowd (a mix of domestic/local nightlife-goers and some tourists) may currently be a more resilient demand base for Lobsteria than inbound international tourists specifically, and that any growth narrative built on "rising tourism" should be qualified by the international-softness trend rather than treated as unambiguous tailwind.
- The six-month seasonality gap (May–November) reported for a comparable nearby restaurant (MiMo district) is a risk factor for any second Miami-area location that is similarly tourist-dependent, and supports exploring more locally-driven/nightlife-driven demand (as Wynwood nightlife itself, independent of tourism, appears to be) over a purely "second tourist corridor" strategy.

- Could not find visitor demographic breakdowns (age, income) specific to nightlife/bar-hopping tourist segments relevant to Wynwood's target customer.

## What do food truck permitting/regulatory requirements look like in Miami-Dade County and nearby municipalities for operating a second truck or opening a second fixed location?

### Takeaway
Permitting is fragmented by jurisdiction (unincorporated Miami-Dade vs. City of Miami/Wynwood vs. Miami Beach vs. Fort Lauderdale/Broward), all require a layered stack of state, county, and local approvals, and costs/restrictions vary sharply — Miami Beach is notably the most restrictive (effectively requiring enclosed permanent buildings outside of permitted events), while unincorporated Miami-Dade's public-property mobile vending permit alone costs roughly $2,850–$3,350 initially plus $2,500–$3,000/year to renew.

### Cited Findings
- In unincorporated Miami-Dade County, each mobile food operator must obtain a Certificate of Use (CU), renewable annually, plus a Mobile Food Vendor permit for vending on public property; the application requires a narrative describing the operation and hours, and a copy of the state food-service license — [Miami-Dade County](https://www.miamidade.gov/permits/mobile-sales.asp)
- Miami-Dade restricts mobile vending to specific zoning districts only: BU, IU, "Urban center," and "Urban area" zones; operating outside designated food-truck zones risks a citation and potential towing — [search synthesis of Miami-Dade permit pages](https://www.miamidade.gov/global/permit.page?Mduid_permit=per1760543400967904)
- Vending on private property (e.g., a parking lot) requires written permission from the property owner on file with the county — [Miami-Dade permit guidance](https://www.miamidade.gov/permits/mobile-sales.asp)
- Estimated Miami-Dade mobile vendor permit cost: ~$3,350 initial fee for on-site cooking (~$2,850 for pre-packaged-only), with annual renewal of ~$3,000 (cooking) or ~$2,500 (pre-packaged) — [search synthesis, multiple permit-guide sites]
- Wynwood specifically falls under City of Miami jurisdiction (not unincorporated Miami-Dade), and City of Miami food truck "gatherings"/roundups require a Temporary Event Permit per site, renewable annually; a single gathering location may host trucks only once per calendar month for a maximum of three consecutive days, and gatherings are strictly prohibited in T3 and T4-R Transect Zones under Miami's Miami 21 zoning code — [City of Miami Ordinance 13796 PDF](https://www.miami.gov/files/assets/public/v/1/document-resources/pdf-docs/ordinance-13796-food-trucks.pdf); [Miami New Times — ordinance](https://www.miaminewtimes.com/food-drink/commission-passes-food-truck-ordinance-what-happens-now-6596720/)
- City of Miami also imposes a 500-foot buffer: food trucks cannot operate within 500 feet of a fixed food-service establishment or a gasoline/combustible-fuel facility, and cannot operate in single-family or some low-density residential zones — [search synthesis of City of Miami ordinance]
- A City of Miami food truck permit reapplication is required whenever the underlying property's ownership changes — relevant given Lobsteria/TCAWBAR's Wynwood site is on a specific parcel — [Miami New Times — Wynwood roundup cancelled](https://www.miaminewtimes.com/food-drink/wynwood-food-truck-roundup-cancelled-brad-knoefler-tattled-6608372/)
- A 2020 Miami Today report noted the Miami City Commission showed limited appetite for passing new, more permissive food-truck regulations, suggesting the regulatory environment for trucks in the City of Miami (which governs Wynwood) has remained relatively restrictive/unchanged for several years — [Miami Today (2020)](https://www.miamitodaynews.com/2020/03/17/miami-lacks-appetite-for-new-food-truck-regulations/)
- Miami Beach is the most restrictive nearby municipality: City Code Section 142-874 requires that business be conducted within "substantially enclosed, permanent buildings," meaning most food trucks in Miami Beach can only legally operate as part of permitted special events or on private lots with specific case-by-case approvals, not as standalone street vendors — [Miami Beach Code PDF](https://docmgmt.miamibeachfl.gov/weblink/0/edoc/194762/06%20Food%20Trucks%20On%20Private%20Property.pdf)
- All Florida mobile food vendors (statewide, so applicable in both Miami-Dade and Broward) must also hold a Florida DBPR Mobile Food Dispensing Vehicle (MFDV) license, and must operate from a licensed commissary kitchen — Florida law explicitly prohibits using a private residence as a commissary — [Florida DACS](https://www.fdacs.gov/Business-Services/Food/Food-Establishments/Mobile-Food-Establishments); [StreetLegal Fort Lauderdale guide](https://streetlegal.io/blog/florida/fort-lauderdale-food-truck-permit-guide)
- Fort Lauderdale/Broward County stack: Mobile Food Vendor License (~$150/yr local), Florida DBPR MFDV license (~$347–$760/yr), sales tax permit, local Business Tax Receipt (~$50–$200/yr), fire safety certification, plus annual health inspection by Broward County Environmental Health Department; trucks are generally confined to commercial/industrial zones and must maintain distance from brick-and-mortar restaurants, with no extended dwell time in one spot — [StreetLegal Fort Lauderdale guide](https://streetlegal.io/blog/florida/fort-lauderdale-food-truck-permit-guide)

### Inferences
- Opening a literal second truck to operate in Wynwood/City of Miami under the existing roundup/gathering framework is constrained (once-per-month, 3-consecutive-day cap per site, annual per-site permit, 500-ft buffer from brick-and-mortar restaurants) — this makes a true "second truck, same neighborhood" model operationally awkward without either a different host site or a shift toward a fixed/semi-fixed private-lot setup.
- Miami Beach's effective ban on standalone street-vending trucks (enclosed-building requirement) means a literal truck-format expansion into Miami Beach is largely foreclosed; any Miami Beach move would likely need to be a brick-and-mortar or private-event-only model, which is a meaningfully different capital/ops commitment than the current Airstream concept.
- Fort Lauderdale/Broward appears comparably or more truck-friendly than Miami Beach (standard commercial-zone operation, lower stated permit costs) and could be an easier regulatory lift for a second truck than Miami Beach, though it still requires the same state DBPR/commissary stack.
- The requirement to re-apply for a City of Miami permit on any change of property ownership at the host site is a latent risk for Lobsteria/TCAWBAR's current Wynwood operation that should be monitored independent of any expansion decision.

- Could not confirm whether Lobsteria/TCAWBAR's existing Wynwood site operates under a "gathering" roundup permit, a private-property lot arrangement, or some other classification — this matters for assessing how a second unit would be licensed, but was not disclosed in any public source found.

## What is the recent trend in Miami's food truck and food delivery market overall?

### Takeaway
The food truck and broader independent-restaurant sector in Miami-Dade is under real stress in 2025 (notable truck/restaurant closures, rising input costs, a widening seasonal demand gap), even as the online food delivery market nationally and in the broader South region continues solid growth — a mixed signal of structural tailwind (delivery) against cyclical headwind (costs, tourism softness) for a delivery-heavy concept like TCAWBAR.

### Cited Findings
- Florida accounts for about 9% of the U.S. national food truck market, with Miami specifically characterized as a "tourism-heavy" market that generates outsized sales through seasonal visitor influxes and outdoor entertainment venues — [search synthesis, food truck trend sources](https://dojobusiness.com/blogs/news/food-truck-industry-growth)
- One industry-adjacent source estimated roughly 900 active food trucks operating in the Miami area, though this figure could not be traced to an official Miami-Dade County government dataset and should be treated as an industry estimate rather than a confirmed count — [search synthesis, no single authoritative primary source found]
- Ms. Cheezious — a grilled-cheese concept that itself began as a food truck and "helped define Miami's food truck boom" — closed its MiMo-district brick-and-mortar location in June 2025 after over a decade, following the sale of the building, illustrating that even iconic, originally truck-based Miami food brands are not immune to 2025 closure pressure — [Miami New Times](https://www.miaminewtimes.com/food-drink/biggest-miami-restaurant-closures-of-2025-40507821/)
- 2025 saw an "unprecedented volume" of Miami restaurant closures spanning small family operators up to Michelin-recognized and high-profile rising-star restaurants, per Miami New Times' closures round-up — [Miami New Times](https://www.miaminewtimes.com/food-drink/biggest-miami-restaurant-closures-of-2025-40507821/)
- Key 2025 cost pressures cited: food costs up 21% in June 2025 (Producer Price Index) — with specific staples like eggs/lettuce rising from ~$35/case to as much as $100/case — plus rising rent, insurance, and labor costs; 91% of independent restaurants nationally raised menu prices in 2024, typically 5–10%, per a James Beard Foundation report — [AOL/Yahoo Finance](https://www.aol.com/finance/florida-restaurants-shutting-down-amid-203500415.html)
- A nearby Miami restaurant (Flora Plant Kitchen, MiMo district) reported a 40% summer-vs-spring 2025 sales drop, with reporting noting the traditional Miami tourist season now effectively ending by May rather than June, creating roughly a six-month underperformance window before December's return of snowbirds/international visitors — [AOL/Yahoo Finance](https://www.aol.com/finance/florida-restaurants-shutting-down-amid-203500415.html)
- On the delivery side: the U.S. online food delivery market is projected to generate $429.9 billion in 2025, growing toward $563.4 billion by 2029; DoorDash holds roughly 67% market share nationally vs. Uber Eats at 23%, and DoorDash's 2025 order volume surpassed $100 billion with net income approaching $1 billion — [search synthesis, Deliverect/Business of Apps/Statista aggregated figures]
- The "South" region of the U.S. (which includes Florida) is cited as one of the fastest-growing geographic segments for online food delivery, at roughly 9.8% CAGR, with reporting attributing this to population growth in Florida, Texas, and Georgia and dense restaurant ecosystems with strong delivery-platform penetration — [search synthesis, delivery market reports]; no source gave a South-Florida-metro-specific (Miami-Dade/Broward only) order-volume figure.
- 2025 national food truck trend reporting (not Miami-specific) emphasizes tech/mobile-ordering adoption, growing catering/event revenue (53% of corporate buyers planning to increase catering budgets; 80% ordering catering at least monthly), rising demand for plant-based menu items (+200% over three years), and increased community/event programming (live music, pop-up markets, brewery partnerships) as revenue diversification strategies — [GoFynd](https://www.goodfynd.com/blogs/future-of-food-trucks-2025-trends); [Accio trend reports](https://www.accio.com/business/trend-in-food-truck)

### Inferences
- The combination of a widening Miami seasonal "dead zone" (roughly May–November) and rising input costs squeezes margin for any new fixed/semi-fixed Miami unit more than it would have two or three years ago; a second Miami-area truck/location should budget conservatively for a multi-month low season rather than assume year-round tourist-driven volume.
- Because national delivery platform growth (and the South region's above-average growth) is a structural tailwind largely independent of local Miami tourism cycles, TCAWBAR's delivery-first model may be comparatively more insulated from the walk-up/tourist-traffic seasonality problem than Lobsteria's late-night, foot-traffic-dependent Airstream model — this asymmetry is worth flagging for any differential growth strategy between the two brands.
- The lack of an official, current Miami-Dade truck count or delivery-order-volume figure specific to South Florida is a genuine data gap in the public record, not just a search-execution limitation; the report-writer should treat any "number of active Miami food trucks" figure as a rough estimate (~900, single secondary source) rather than a verified statistic.

### Gaps
- No South-Florida-specific (Miami-Dade/Broward/Palm Beach) food delivery order-volume or revenue growth figures were found; all delivery-market figures available are national or multi-state-regional ("South region") only.
- No official, sourced count of currently active/licensed food trucks in Miami-Dade County, City of Miami, or Broward County was found — the ~900 figure is a single, unverified secondary-source estimate.
- No data found specifically isolating "food truck" closures (vs. brick-and-mortar restaurant closures) in the 2025 Miami closure wave, beyond the Ms. Cheezious example (which itself had become a brick-and-mortar by the time it closed).

## Are there other South Florida/Florida cities or neighborhoods with a demographic/tourism profile similar to Wynwood for a logical second location?

### Takeaway
Among the comparable nightlife/dining districts researched, none fully replicates Wynwood's combination of youthful art-district nightlife, moderate (not ultra-high) incomes, and tourist-plus-local crowd; the closest analogues on tourism/nightlife density are South Beach/Miami Beach (more restrictive truck regulation) and Las Olas/Fort Lauderdale or Atlantic Avenue/Delray Beach (both skew notably older and more affluent, with a more "upscale early-evening dining" character than Wynwood's late-night, younger crowd).

### Cited Findings
- Wynwood demographics: population ~7,688, median age 37, ethnic composition ~70% Hispanic/16% White/11% Black, with median household income reported inconsistently across sources (one source: $43,036; another: $72,646) — [Point2Homes](https://www.point2homes.com/US/Neighborhood/FL/Wynwood-Demographics.html); [BestNeighborhood.org](https://bestneighborhood.org/household-income-wynwood-miami-fl/)
- Wynwood's draw is explicitly art/culture/nightlife-driven: Wynwood Walls alone has 75+ exhibition spaces, surrounded by bars, clubs, cafes and boutique shops marketed to both residents and tourists — [LuxeMiamiApartments](https://luxemiamiapartments.com/neighborhoods/wynwood)
- Las Olas (Fort Lauderdale) comparison: population only ~2,731, median age 60, average individual income $157,045 — explicitly described by one source as nightlife "for the over-50 crowd," with a dining scene skewing toward steakhouses/seafood and relaxed upscale cocktail lounges rather than Wynwood-style late-night youth nightlife — [Proximitii](https://www.proximitii.com/usa/fl/fort+lauderdale/las+olas+isles/); [The Traveler](https://www.thetraveler.org/las-olas-beach-nightlife-guide-bars-rooftops-after-dark-tips/)
- Delray Beach/Atlantic Avenue comparison: broader Delray Beach population 121,353, median age 58.4, average individual income $60,493, median household income ~$69,800, ~45% bachelor's-degree-or-higher; nightlife peaks in winter months with street festivals, and is explicitly branded as a "Village by the Sea" attracting "upscale visitors from all over the world," 53 miles north of Miami — [SellsSouthFlorida](https://sellssouthflorida.com/neighborhoods/delray-beach); [I Love Delray Beach](https://www.i-love-delray-beach.com/award-winning-atlantic-avenue-in-delray-beach-florida.html)
- Brickell vs. Design District comparison (both within Miami-Dade, candidates closer to Wynwood): Brickell has average individual income $101,989 and a genuine nightclub/bar-dense scene comparable in density to Wynwood/South Beach; Design District has a much smaller resident population (~457) and average individual income of $59,106, and is explicitly described as "dining-driven rather than club-driven," not a nightclub destination like Wynwood or South Beach — [AllInMiami](https://www.allinmiami.com/blog/design-district-miami-faqs); [search synthesis of neighborhood-guide income data]
- Miami Beach/South Beach already has at least one established lobster-focused brand (The Lobster Shack, South Pointe) and a well-known seafood destination (Lure Fishbar), indicating some existing competitive presence in that specific category if Miami Beach were chosen as a second site — [Lobster Shack Miami](https://lobstershackmiami.com/)
- Miami Beach's food-truck-hostile zoning (enclosed-building requirement, Code Sec. 142-874) was already identified above as a major practical barrier to replicating Lobsteria's Airstream format there without converting to brick-and-mortar.

### Inferences
- Brickell is the Miami-Dade neighborhood that most closely matches Wynwood's nightlife density and bar/club culture while having a notably higher average income ($101,989 vs. Wynwood's ~$43k–73k range) — this could support Lobsteria's higher price points ($14–75+) even better than Wynwood itself, and no lobster-roll-specific competitor was identified there in this research, though it was not exhaustively checked.
- Las Olas (Fort Lauderdale) and Atlantic Avenue (Delray Beach) are tourism-dense and have strong dining scenes, but their demographic skew (median age ~58-60, over-50-oriented nightlife) suggests they are a mismatch for Lobsteria's apparent late-night, younger-skewing Wynwood crowd; they may be better suited to a more upscale, early-evening sit-down format than to a late-night Airstream truck, if pursued at all.
- Design District's "dining-driven, not club-driven" character and small residential base make it a weaker match for a late-night (5pm–2am) walk-up truck model than for a reservation-style sit-down restaurant, and is probably a lower-priority candidate for literal truck replication versus Brickell.
- Given Fort Lauderdale/Broward's comparatively truck-friendlier regulatory stack (vs. Miami Beach's de facto truck ban), if a second-city (not second-Miami-neighborhood) move is preferred, Fort Lauderdale is likely the more operationally feasible first external market to test among those researched, notwithstanding its older demographic skew which may suit TCAWBAR (daytime/early-evening, less reliant on a 20s-30s late-night crowd) better than Lobsteria.

### Gaps
- No current competitor-density check specific to Brickell for either lobster rolls or crepes/waffles was performed (time/tool-budget constraint); this should be a priority follow-up before recommending Brickell as a second-location candidate.
- No Census-tract-level or more rigorous demographic dataset (e.g., American Community Survey figures) was retrieved for any neighborhood — all figures above come from real-estate/neighborhood-guide aggregator sites (Point2Homes, BestNeighborhood.org, Proximitii, etc.) of unverified methodology, and sources on Wynwood's own median household income directly conflict ($43,036 vs. $72,646), so neighborhood income figures throughout this section should be treated as directional, not precise.
- No comparable nightlife-density or tourism-visitor data was found for West Palm Beach (as distinct from Delray Beach), despite Palm Beach being named as a candidate market in the assignment; this is a gap for the report-writer to flag or address via a separate pass if time allows.
