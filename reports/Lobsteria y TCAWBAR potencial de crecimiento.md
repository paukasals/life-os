# Lobster rolls prove scalable; crepes stay unproven

Lobster rolls pass the Alberto/Glovo-style test and crepes & waffles do not — not because crepes lack demand, but because the specific delivery-first execution TCAWBAR runs has no proven comparable anywhere in the public record. For **Lobsteria**, independent sources converge cleanly: menu penetration is up **52% in four years** ([NRN/Datassential](https://www.nrn.com/menu-trends/lobster-rolls-trending-in-restaurants)), two national chains have already proven a food-truck-to-85-unit franchise playbook with ~**$1.3M average unit sales** ([sharpsheets.io FDD summary](https://sharpsheets.io/blog/cousins-maine-lobster-franchise-sales-costs-profits/)), and restaurant-level pricing power has held even as wholesale lobster costs swung wildly ([Robb Report](https://robbreport.com/food-drink/dining/lobster-roll-prices-remain-high-lobster-prices-plummet-1234737916/)) — a real, bounded, data-backed growth category. For **TCAWBAR**, the category-level demand signal is also genuinely strong — creperies and waffle shops ranked among the fastest-growing new-business categories nationally in 2023–2024, at **+29% and +32% YoY** respectively ([Yelp State of the Restaurant Industry 2024](https://trends.yelp.com/state-of-the-restaurant-industry-2024.html)) — but every brand that has scaled that demand into multiple units did it dine-in-first, and the one brand that tried a delivery-first ghost-kitchen variant of the category is shrinking, not growing ([FDD Exchange](https://fddexchange.com/fdd/crepe-delicious-2025/)). Neither UberEats, DoorDash, nor Grubhub publishes category-level frequency, retention, or repeat-purchase data for seafood, desserts, or anything else — the exact proprietary metric Alberto used inside Glovo to pick categories worth scaling nationally — so this report states plainly where that gap sits and what public proxies had to stand in for it. The bottom line: Lobsteria's path to a second unit is backed by a demonstrated national playbook; TCAWBAR's path rests on real but unproven category momentum that no one has yet converted into a delivery-first multi-unit business.

## Lobster rolls ride a proven, bounded national growth curve

Every available data point on lobster rolls points the same direction — growing, not flat or declining — and that consistency across independent source types (a menu-data firm, a trade association, company press releases, trade journalism) is itself the strongest evidence in this research. Datassential's MenuTrends data, covering over a million menu items across 7,000+ restaurants, shows lobster rolls now on **2% of all sandwich-offering menus nationally** (6% at fine dining, 3% at casual dining), up **52% from four years ago** ([NRN](https://www.nrn.com/menu-trends/lobster-rolls-trending-in-restaurants)). The National Restaurant Association's 2026 "What's Hot" forecast names seafood broadly a "clear winner" for the year ([RestaurantDive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/nra-whats-hot-2026-menu-trends-report-smash-burgers/806159/)), and the dish has crossed from specialist concepts into mainstream chains — Panera Bread and Friendly's both ran lobster-roll LTOs in 2025–2026 ([Restaurant Business Online](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/food/surf-or-turf-lobster-steak-share-menu-spotlight)), a classic signal that a dish has moved from niche to broadly recognized trend. Crucially, the category has already proven it can scale past a single truck: **Cousins Maine Lobster** grew from one truck in 2012 to **85+ units across 35 states by April 2025**, targeting 100 units by year-end, with 89% of units franchised and average 2024 truck sales of roughly **$1.3 million** ([Undercurrent News](https://www.undercurrentnews.com/2025/04/30/us-lobster-roll-truck-chain-surpasses-85-units-with-continued-growth-plans/); [sharpsheets.io](https://sharpsheets.io/blog/cousins-maine-lobster-franchise-sales-costs-profits/)), and the brand surpassed **$1 billion in cumulative systemwide sales** by mid-2025 ([Press Herald](https://www.pressherald.com/2025/06/26/cousins-maine-lobster-has-surpassed-1-billion-in-systemwide-sales/)). **Mason's Famous Lobster Rolls** has taken the brick-and-mortar path to a similar result — roughly 35 US locations plus active expansion into Europe and the Middle East ([QSR Magazine](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/news/masons-famous-lobster-rolls-to-grow-in-middle-east/); [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/garystern/2025/10/15/the-secret-behind-masons-famous-lobster-rolls-success/)). This is a double-to-low-triple-digit-unit ceiling, not a McDonald's-scale opportunity — the category never appears on more than 6% of menus even at its strongest segment — but it is a real, demonstrated, franchise-validated ceiling, and the food-truck format specifically (Lobsteria's current format) is the more battle-tested path to that scale than brick-and-mortar.

The one real structural risk is on the supply side, not the demand side. Maine dock lobster prices swung between roughly **$4 and $7 per pound** from 2021 to 2025 while landed volume fell for four consecutive years to a 15-year low in 2024 ([Maine Public](https://www.mainepublic.org/business-and-economy/2025-02-28/maine-lobster-landings-hit-a-15-year-low-in-2024); [Maine DMR](https://www.maine.gov/dmr/news/fri-03062026-1200-2025-maine-commercial-fisheries-value-again-tops-600-million)), and 2025 tariff policy was explicitly cited as adding fresh market uncertainty ([National Fisherman](https://www.nationalfisherman.com/maine-lobster-landings-fall-for-fourth-straight-year)). But operators have shown real pricing power against that volatility: Robb Report's explicit finding is that "lobster roll prices are soaring, even as the price of lobster plummets" — The Clam Shack's roll rose 58% from 2020 to 2022 while Cousins' average nationwide price climbed from roughly $17.50 to $21–22 over the same window ([Robb Report](https://robbreport.com/food-drink/dining/lobster-roll-prices-remain-high-lobster-prices-plummet-1234737916/)). Lobsteria's stated $14–75+ price band sits well above both chains' $18–22 core pricing, which cuts two ways: it gives more margin headroom to absorb raw-material swings, and it also means Lobsteria faces almost no direct competition at its own top end in Miami — the local competitive set (Cousins' Miami/Pompano Beach trucks, pricing its Maine Roll at $22; Mason's Downtown Miami location; independent operators like The Lobsta Guy and Lobsta Rolls) all clusters in the $18–22 range, well below Lobsteria's ceiling ([Cousins Maine Lobster menu via DoorDash](https://www.doordash.com/business/cousins-maine-lobster-11173524/menu/); [Yelp Pompano Beach](https://www.yelp.com/biz/cousins-maine-lobster-pompano-beach)). **Verdict for Lobsteria: yes, the category has real, data-backed room to grow.** The demand signal is consistent and multi-sourced, a national playbook already exists for taking a lobster-roll truck to dozens of units, and Lobsteria's premium positioning avoids the most crowded price tier locally. The constraints are not demand-side but execution-side: input-cost volatility, Wynwood's restrictive truck-gathering permitting (a 500-foot buffer from fixed restaurants and a once-per-month, three-day cap per site under City of Miami's ordinance — [Ordinance 13796](https://www.miami.gov/files/assets/public/v/1/document-resources/pdf-docs/ordinance-13796-food-trucks.pdf)), and Miami's softening international-tourist base.

## Crepes and waffles are genuinely trending — but no one has proven the delivery-first model

The crepes-and-waffles category clears the same first bar lobster rolls do: real, triangulated, multi-source evidence of accelerating demand. Yelp's 2024 State of the Restaurant Industry report, tracking actual new-business openings from May 2023 to April 2024, found **waffle-concept openings up 32% and creperie openings up 29%** year-over-year — both ahead of ice cream/frozen yogurt (22%) and donuts (17%) ([Yelp](https://trends.yelp.com/state-of-the-restaurant-industry-2024.html)). The 2024 Technomic Breakfast Study found waffles up **15.4% on restaurant appetizer menus** versus 2023, with 40% of consumers saying they're more likely to order a waffle than any other breakfast item ([NRN coverage](https://www.nrn.com/supplier-news/explore-the-endless-opportunities-of-all-day-breakfast)), and Liège waffles specifically — the "bar"-style format most relevant to TCAWBAR — grew **39% in foodservice locations** over the prior year ([The Kitchn](https://www.thekitchn.com/liege-waffles-grocery-trends-23602709)). Unlike sushi circa 2000, which needed the California roll as a de-risking "training wheel" before Americans would try raw fish, crepes and waffles need essentially no category education: waffles are already one of the most familiar foods in America, and crepes are a known concept even to people who rarely order one, so the growth constraint here is discovery and habitual-ordering-occasion formation, not basic unfamiliarity. The ghost-kitchen/virtual-brand ecosystem also structurally favors commodity dessert concepts — Ghost Kitchens International runs Cinnabon and Ben & Jerry's alongside other virtual brands and has expanded into 30 Walmart locations ([SupermarketNews](https://www.supermarketnews.com/foodservice-retail/ghost-kitchen-brands-makes-its-walmart-debut)) — which is directionally favorable for a delivery-first dessert concept's basic channel fit.

The problem is that none of this translates into a proven comparable for what TCAWBAR actually is. Every multi-unit crepe/waffle brand identified in this research is dine-in, fast-casual, or kiosk-first: **Sweet Paris Creperie & Café**, the one clear growth story in the category (16–27 units depending on count date, a stated 100-unit/8-year target, and active Miami-Dade expansion with three locations opened in roughly three years — [RestaurantDive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/franchise-spotlight-sweet-paris-creperie-aims-for-100-units/642664/); [QSR Magazine](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/news/sweet-paris-creperie-cafe-opens-third-miami-based-shop/)) — operates as a sit-down café. **Bruxie**, **CoCo Crêpes**, and **Wafels & Dinges** are all walk-up, kiosk, or dine-in concepts, with Wafels & Dinges notably waiting roughly 15 years before even attempting franchising ([RestaurantNews.com](https://www.restaurantnews.com/iconic-nyc-belgian-waffle-institution-wafels-dinges-to-begin-selective-franchising-nationwide-031122/)). The single brand that did build an explicitly delivery-first ghost-kitchen variant — **Crepe Delicious By Ghost Kitchens** — belongs to a parent chain that has contracted from "over 65 locations globally" to just 11 total units (9 in the US) per its most recent FDD ([FDD Exchange](https://fddexchange.com/fdd/crepe-delicious-2025/)), which is a shrinking brand, not a growing one. No platform report from DoorDash, Uber Eats, or Grubhub names "waffles" or "crepes" as a breakout delivery-order category the way it has named cuisines like African food (+61% YoY) or items like matcha; the absence is itself informative, since a category with real delivery-platform momentum typically gets called out by name in exactly this kind of trend journalism. And the growth itself is concentrated in a narrow 2023–2025 window tied to a broader "shareable treat economy" (TikTok-driven discovery of visually distinctive foods), which RestaurantDive's own coverage explicitly frames as an open question — "are they just a fad?" ([RestaurantDive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/dessert-concepts-seeing-record-growth-but-are-they-just-a-fad/723871/)) — rather than a settled decade-long structural trend. **Verdict for TCAWBAR: the underlying category has real, demonstrated growth, but that is a different claim from "TCAWBAR's business model has real growth potential," and the data cannot bridge that gap.** No comparable delivery-first crepes/waffles brand has scaled past the one shrinking example found. This is not a disguised "no" — the demand-side signal is too strong and too consistent to call this category a dead end — but it is an honest "unproven," and it is the single clearest point in this research where Lobsteria and TCAWBAR diverge.

## Where Glovo's internal data wins and public data cannot follow

The method this report is trying to mirror — category-level frequency, retention, and conversion data inside a delivery platform's own order book — simply does not exist in public form for either category, and this is the most consistent finding across every research note pulled. DoorDash and Uber Eats disclose plenty of granular statistics: cuisine-level year-over-year growth (African cuisine orders up 61% YoY — [DoorDash newsroom](https://about.doordash.com/en-us/news/americas-favorite-delivery-spots-on-doordash-2025)), company-wide order volume (DoorDash's total orders up 23% YoY to 3.2 billion in 2025 — [DoorDash 8-K via SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1792789/000179278926000012/prodq4dashex991-pressrelea.htm)), and broad-vertical engagement metrics (frequency gains in grocery/retail "New Verticals," not food type — [FreightWaves](https://www.freightwaves.com/news/a-tale-of-2-food-apps-uber-eats-grows-doordash-finds-new-verticals)). What neither platform, nor Grubhub, has ever published is a repeat-purchase rate, days-between-orders figure, or conversion rate broken out by food category — seafood vs. dessert or any other pairing. This gap is structural, not a research-session failure: these platforms report granular data on cuisines and viral items because that content drives marketing and merchant-acquisition narratives, but category-level frequency/retention is exactly the kind of operationally sensitive data a platform would keep proprietary for its own category-investment decisions — precisely the asset Alberto had access to at Glovo and that this research cannot replicate from outside.

In its absence, the best available public proxies diverge in reliability by question. For **category-level demand growth**, menu-penetration data (Datassential) and new-business-opening data (Yelp) are genuinely solid, named, dated, and triangulated proxies — strong enough to support the growth verdicts above with real confidence. For **delivery-specific channel fit**, the proxies are much weaker: food-journalism consensus that fried/breaded seafood "doesn't travel well" and that ice cream/temperature-sensitive desserts also carry documented complaint risk is anecdotal and self-selected, not a rigorous complaint-rate dataset, and should be read as "directionally consistent color," not data. One clean, confirmed finding that does hold up: **published commission structures on both platforms are tiered by service-plan level (15–30%+ depending on tier), not by food category** ([Zay-OS](https://zay-os.com/restaurant-delivery-commission-statistics); [Food On Demand](https://foodondemand.com/04012026/how-the-uber-eats-fees-stack-up-against-3pd-competitors/)), so neither Lobsteria nor TCAWBAR gets a built-in platform-economics advantage or penalty from category alone — any difference in their delivery unit economics will come from ticket size, packaging/spoilage cost, and actual order frequency, none of which is publicly disclosed by category. Any number this report could offer for "seafood order frequency vs. dessert order frequency" would be invented, not sourced, and is deliberately omitted rather than approximated.

## Miami's climate, permitting, and tourism cycle decide more than the national category data does

Both brands' practical growth path runs through Miami-specific constraints that the national category data cannot see. Miami-Dade tourism hit a record **28.3 million visitors and $32.2 billion in economic impact in 2025** ([GMCVB via Hotel News Resource](https://www.hotelnewsresource.com/article141536.html)), but that record was achieved only because domestic Florida-resident visitation offset a real decline in international arrivals — Colombia arrivals fell 12%, Dominican Republic 20%, Canada 8% — driven by new federal registration rules for long-stay visitors, tariff-related Canadian pullback, and a slowing global economy ([WLRN](https://www.wlrn.org/business/2025-11-07/miami-dade-tourism)). A nearby MiMo-district restaurant reported a **40% sales drop in summer 2025 versus spring**, with the traditional tourist season now effectively ending by May instead of June, opening a roughly six-month low-volume window before snowbirds return in December ([AOL/Yahoo Finance](https://www.aol.com/finance/florida-restaurants-shutting-down-amid-203500415.html)). This cuts differently for the two brands: Lobsteria's late-night, foot-traffic-dependent Airstream format is more exposed to that tourism-cycle risk, while TCAWBAR's delivery-first model rides on top of a structural tailwind largely independent of walk-up tourist traffic — Uber Eats alone generated **$13.7 billion in 2024 revenue (+13.2% YoY)** on gross bookings projected to reach **$90.8 billion in 2025** ([Business of Apps](https://www.businessofapps.com/data/uber-eats-statistics/)), and DoorDash's total orders grew **23% YoY to 3.2 billion in 2025** ([DoorDash 8-K via SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1792789/000179278926000012/prodq4dashex991-pressrelea.htm)) — a genuine structural asymmetry in TCAWBAR's favor even though its category-fit question remains open. The precise magnitude of that tailwind specific to Florida's delivery market could not be sourced to a traceable, named figure in this research and is deliberately left unquantified here rather than cited loosely.

Permitting works against a simple "add a second truck in the same neighborhood" plan for Lobsteria specifically. Wynwood falls under City of Miami jurisdiction, where food-truck gatherings require a per-site Temporary Event Permit, are capped at once per calendar month for a maximum of three consecutive days per location, and must sit at least 500 feet from any fixed food-service establishment ([Ordinance 13796](https://www.miami.gov/files/assets/public/v/1/document-resources/pdf-docs/ordinance-13796-food-trucks.pdf)) — a framework built for rotating truck roundups, not a second permanent unit in the same district. Miami Beach is flatly more hostile: City Code Section 142-874 requires business be conducted inside "substantially enclosed, permanent buildings," which effectively forecloses a literal Airstream-format expansion there without converting to brick-and-mortar ([Miami Beach Code](https://docmgmt.miamibeachfl.gov/weblink/0/edoc/194762/06%20Food%20Trucks%20On%20Private%20Property.pdf)). Broward/Fort Lauderdale is comparably more truck-friendly on paper, and Brickell stands out among Miami-Dade neighborhoods as the closest demographic match to Wynwood's nightlife density with a notably higher average income (~$101,989 vs. Wynwood's ~$43k–73k range) and no identified lobster-roll competitor — a plausible, if unverified, next-site candidate ([AllInMiami](https://www.allinmiami.com/blog/design-district-miami-faqs)). One structural fact ties both brands together regardless of path chosen: Lobsteria and TCAWBAR already **share the same Wynwood address**, meaning any "second location" decision for either brand is really a decision about whether to replicate the two-brand bundling model elsewhere or split the brands into separate footprints — a strategic choice the national category data cannot make for the founders, but one the local permitting and competitive data makes concrete.

## Conclusion

The honest, separated verdict: Lobsteria's category has cleared the bar Alberto would have wanted to see at Glovo — consistent, multi-sourced demand growth plus an existing national unit-economics playbook (Cousins Maine Lobster, Mason's) that proves a premium lobster-roll truck can scale past one location without inventing a new business model. TCAWBAR's category is growing just as convincingly on the demand side, but it is scaling into an execution model — delivery-first — that no comparable brand in the category has yet made work at any real scale, which means the open question is not "does anyone want crepes and waffles" (yes) but "will this specific distribution model capture that demand" (unknown, and unknowable from public data alone). The deeper takeaway for how this kind of analysis should be run going forward is that public proxies are genuinely good at answering the demand-side half of Alberto's framework — is the category growing, is it becoming mainstream, has anyone proven a scaling playbook — but structurally cannot answer the supply-side half he had at Glovo: frequency, retention, and conversion by category inside the delivery funnel itself. Any national expansion decision for either brand that leans on delivery economics specifically should treat that absence as a real blind spot to close with the brands' own UberEats/DoorDash dashboard data — their own order history is, in fact, the one dataset that could answer the Glovo-style question directly, and it is sitting inside their existing Wynwood operation already.
