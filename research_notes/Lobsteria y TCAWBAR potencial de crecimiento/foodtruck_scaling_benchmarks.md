# Food Truck → Multi-Unit / Franchise Scaling Benchmarks (US)

## What is the typical failure/success rate for food trucks attempting to scale beyond one unit?

### Takeaway
There is no single authoritative statistic on "trucks that tried to scale beyond one unit and failed" — no trade association or SBA dataset tracks that specific transition. What exists is (a) general food-truck survival data, (b) general restaurant/food-service survival data, and (c) general franchise survival data, all of which point the same direction: mobile food is already higher-failure than fixed restaurants in the first few years, and the jump to a second unit is a distinct, capital-intensive decision point that most single-truck operators never reach — most industry guidance explicitly tells operators not to add a second truck until the first is "consistently profitable" and maxed out on capacity. Independent operators still control the large majority (62–86%, depending on source) of the ~92,000-truck US market, implying multi-unit/franchise scaling is the exception, not the norm.

### Cited Findings
- About 15–20% of food trucks fail within the first year, and 30–40% close within 3 years, per one aggregator synthesis; roughly 50–55% are still operating after 5 years, vs. ~40% of traditional restaurants — though other sources cite a much higher ~60% first-year failure figure, indicating real disagreement in the data. — [Toast/aggregator synthesis via search](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/on-the-line/food-truck-success-rate)
- In the broader accommodation/food-services sector, BLS Business Employment Dynamics data shows 82.5% of firms survive year 1, 55.3% survive to year 5, and 38.3% survive to year 10. — [BLS-sourced synthesis](https://www.restroworks.com/blog/restaurant-success-statistics/)
- Real first-year restaurant failure is 14–17% per BLS and UC Berkeley research — the commonly cited "90% fail in year one" figure is a myth. — [DirectOrders/QSR.pro synthesis of BLS/Berkeley data](https://www.directorders.com/blog/restaurant-failure-rate)
- Franchise 5-year failure rates run roughly 20–25%, vs. ~50% for independent small businesses in some syntheses, but other data is far less rosy: Timothy Bates's academic study tracking 20,500+ firms started 1984–1987 found only 65.3% of franchises were still operating after 4 years, vs. 72% of independent businesses — i.e., franchising did not outperform independents in that dataset. — [Entrepreneur-cited synthesis](https://www.entrepreneur.com/franchises/we-crunched-5-years-of-franchise-industry-data-here-are-4/444955); [Moyak/Bates study synthesis](https://www.moyak.com/papers/franchising-failures.html)
- Franchise concepts with startup costs under $25,000 show much higher failure/termination rates (9.3% for $15K–$25K band) than those above $25,000 (avg. failure <5%) — relevant because food-truck franchise entry costs often sit right at this threshold. — [Entrepreneur-sourced synthesis](https://www.entrepreneur.com/franchises/we-crunched-5-years-of-franchise-industry-data-here-are-4/444955)
- As of 2025, independent operators still represent the large majority of the ~92,257-truck US food truck market (estimates range 62.1% per Mordor Intelligence to 85.6% per another industry estimate), with franchise/chain concepts growing faster (7.9%–12.15% CAGR) off a small base — meaning multi-unit/franchised food trucks remain a minority segment even as they outgrow independents. — [Mordor Intelligence / industry synthesis](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/united-states-food-truck-market); [gofoodservice.com synthesis](https://www.gofoodservice.com/blog/food-trucks-matter)
- The US food truck industry is nearing $3 billion in annual revenue across 92,000+ businesses (IBISWorld, reported via CNBC Nov 2025), but 2025 revenue growth slowed to just 0.2% even as unit count grew ~17%, with inflation in ingredient/transport costs, permitting, and logistics squeezing margins — a sign the environment for scaling is getting harder, not easier. — [CNBC, Nov 2025](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/11/01/food-truck-restaurant-billion-dollar-business-keys-success-costs.html)
- Standard operator guidance: profits should go toward maintenance reserves, menu improvement, and marketing — not a second truck — until the first truck is "consistently profitable"; a second truck is typically only pursued once the first truck is maxed out on capacity and turning down business. — [Industry guidance synthesis](https://www.wnyfoodtrucks.com/avoid-these-critical-mistakes-that-cause-food-trucks-to-fail/); [Food truck financing guide](https://clarifycapital.com/blog/food-truck-financing)

### Inferences
- The absence of a clean "% of trucks that scale successfully" statistic is itself a finding: this is a long-tail event industry-wide, not a well-tracked outcome, which should be communicated to the client as a data gap rather than papered over.
- Given restaurant 5-year survival (~50-55%) and franchise 4-year survival in the ~65% range even for established systems, a realistic planning assumption is that fewer than half of single-unit concepts that attempt a second unit/franchise push will still be operating in that expanded form 4-5 years later — and that's for concepts that already cleared the harder bar of surviving as a single unit.
- Rising ingredient/logistics costs industry-wide (2025 data) argue for stress-testing unit economics at a second location before committing capital, since margin compression is a current, not hypothetical, risk.

### Gaps
- No source found gives a clean, named statistic specifically for "% of single-unit food trucks that attempt to add a 2nd truck/location and succeed vs. fail." This appears to not be tracked by any food truck association (e.g., no NFTA-branded statistic was found) or SBA report — flagging this as unavailable rather than inventing a number.
- Could not verify whether a "National Food Truck Association" publishes its own failure-rate statistics; search results did not surface a primary NFTA publication with this data (most-cited stats trace to aggregator/blog sites synthesizing BLS and IBISWorld data, not direct primary association reports).

## Named case studies: food trucks that scaled to multiple units or franchised nationally (timeline, funding, replicability, current status)

### Takeaway
The clearest "textbook" success is Cousins Maine Lobster (truck → national franchise in ~2 years, Shark Tank-funded awareness, commissary/centralized-supply model, 85+ units by 2025); Luke's Lobster shows the brick-and-mortar-from-day-one path to the same category; Coolhaus and Kogi show "successful growth that later re-shaped or partially retrenched" rather than clean linear franchise scaling; and The Grilled Cheese Truck is a clear cautionary tale of franchise ambitions collapsing into fraud. Across all of them, a centralized commissary/supply-chain function appears as early as possible, well before unit count justifies it on its own.

### Cited Findings

**Cousins Maine Lobster (lobster rolls — most directly comparable case study)**
- Founded by cousins Sabin Lomac and Jim Tselikis; first truck launched in Los Angeles in April 2012. — [Search synthesis of multiple sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cousins_Maine_Lobster)
- Appeared on Shark Tank in 2012 and received a $55,000 investment from Barbara Corcoran for a 15% stake — a capital/credibility injection, not primarily a funding source for truck-building capital. — [Shark Tank Blog synthesis](https://www.sharktankblog.com/55k-to-585m-shark-tanks-biggest-flip/)
- Began franchising in 2014 — roughly 2 years after the first truck launched. By 2016 the company had 13 locations/20 trucks and opened its first brick-and-mortar restaurant in LA. — [Search synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cousins_Maine_Lobster)
- By April 2025 the chain surpassed 85 units across roughly 35 states, with plans to open 30+ more trucks in 2026; 2024 average truck sales were approximately $1.3 million per unit. — [Undercurrent News, Apr 2025](https://www.undercurrentnews.com/2025/04/30/us-lobster-roll-truck-chain-surpasses-85-units-with-continued-growth-plans/); [QSR Magazine, 100th location by end of 2025](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/news/cousins-maine-lobster-to-open-100th-location-by-end-of-2025/)
- Systemwide sales surpassed $1 billion cumulatively since 2012 as of mid/late-2025 announcements. — [PR Newswire, 2025](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/cousins-maine-lobster-celebrates-monumental-milestone-over-1-billion-in-systemwide-sales-302491463.html)
- Franchise economics: $40,000 franchise fee; total investment for a food truck unit ranges $194,000–$644,700 (including $90,000–$140,000 payable to the franchisor/affiliates); brick-and-mortar/storefront investment is $267,000–$969,000; ongoing fees are an 8% royalty + 2% marketing fee on the truck model. Reported average unit gross sales of ~$1,241,105 with estimated owner earnings of $124,111–$148,933. — [Franchise FDD data synthesis](https://sharpsheets.io/blog/cousins-maine-lobster-franchise-sales-costs-profits/); [vettedbiz.com FDD synthesis](https://www.vettedbiz.com/franchises/cousins-maine-lobster-food-truck)
- Replicability mechanism: a centralized national supply chain plans lobster volume years/quarters/months/weeks in advance so that Raleigh, Charlotte, Columbus, and Pittsburgh franchisees all receive product the same way; franchisees then run their own local commissary and must buy food, beverages, equipment, and branded fixtures from company-designated suppliers; trucks are built to company blueprints/specs by an approved truck builder (Premier Food Trucks built the company's 40th mobile kitchen). — [Food Truck Operator/NRN/QSR synthesis](https://www.foodtruckoperator.com/articles/cousins-maine-lobster-keeps-growth-churning-through-authenticity/); [QSR Magazine, Premier Food Trucks 40th build](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/news/premier-food-trucks-completes-40th-mobile-kitchen-for-cousins-maine-lobster/)
- Notably, many Cousins franchisees deliberately choose the food-truck format over brick-and-mortar first because of lower investment and faster path to cash flow — a direct, relevant data point for a premium lobster roll truck considering its own expansion path. — [Nation's Restaurant News](https://www.nrn.com/restaurant-franchising/why-these-cousins-maine-lobster-franchisees-went-food-truck-first-instead-of-brick-and-mortar)

**Luke's Lobster (lobster rolls — brick-and-mortar-first comparable, not truck-origin, but same product category and same "how do you scale a perishable premium seafood product nationally" problem)**
- Founded 2009 by Luke Holden and Ben Conniff; first "shack" in Manhattan's East Village; a second NYC location opened May 2010. — [CNBC/Forbes synthesis](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/07/02/how-the-founders-of-lukes-lobster-built-a-global-food-business.html)
- Grew from that single East Village shack to 35+ additional locations across the US and internationally (Japan, Singapore), with shacks now in roughly 12 US states. — [QSR Magazine synthesis](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/growth/emerging-concepts/luke-s-lobster/)
- Critical replicability move: in 2012 the company built its own seafood processing company (Cape Seafood) in Saco, Maine — i.e., vertically integrated its own commissary/processing facility rather than relying on each location to source and prep whole lobster independently. — [Search synthesis](https://www.qsrweb.com/blogs/lukes-lobster-surpasses-10-year-milestone-delivering-sustainable-seafood/)
- In 2018, expanded into retail/CPG (branded frozen lobster meat and seafood meals in grocery stores), and launched a direct-to-consumer online market in 2020 — diversifying revenue beyond the shack model. — [SeafoodSource](https://www.seafoodsource.com/news/foodservice-retail/luke-s-lobster-makes-foray-into-retail-with-two-new-offerings-at-whole-foods)
- Status: Still operating as a growing multi-channel seafood business (shacks + retail + e-commerce) as of most recent reporting found.

**Kogi BBQ (Roy Choi — Korean-Mexican fusion truck, LA)**
- Kogi's truck fleet has remained small and intentionally so: it "typically fluctuates between 4 to 5 trucks" even years after its 2008-2009 launch and viral growth via Twitter — it did not scale the truck fleet broadly. — [Search synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kogi_Korean_BBQ)
- Instead of multiplying trucks, founder Roy Choi diversified into a portfolio of distinct brick-and-mortar concepts: Chego!, POT, Commissary (at The Line Hotel), A-Frame, and eventually a Kogi-branded brick-and-mortar (Kogi Taqueria in a Palms strip mall), plus an "Alibi Room" venue in Culver City where the truck parks each evening. — [LAist](https://laist.com/news/food/kogi-palms); [Time Out LA](https://www.timeout.com/los-angeles/blog/rejoice-la-is-finally-getting-a-kogi-brick-and-mortar-010516)
- Remains a privately held partnership; has also moved into airport concessions as another growth channel distinct from truck replication. — [Search synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kogi_Korean_BBQ)
- **Interpretation for this report:** Kogi is a case of a founder choosing portfolio/brand diversification over truck-fleet or franchise replication — a valid but distinct growth path from "scale the same concept to many units."

**Coolhaus (architecturally-themed ice cream sandwiches — truck-to-retail/CPG, LA)**
- Founded 2009 by Natasha Case and Freya Estreller; launched with a $2,500–$2,900 used postal truck bought via Craigslist/personal credit card, debuting at Coachella 2009 to a crowd of 100,000. — [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/08/16/how-coolhaus-ice-cream-went-from-one-food-truck-to-millions-in-sales.html); [Gusto](https://gusto.com/resources/articles/business-growth/entrepreneur-advice/coolhaus-growth-strategy)
- Scaled primarily via retail/CPG partnerships rather than truck replication: began selling prepackaged product to supermarkets in 2010, opened first storefront in 2011, and over roughly 3 years built partnerships with Whole Foods, Urban Outfitters, and Quicksilver, reaching over 6,000 (later 7,500+) grocery stores nationally; at peak operated ~10 mobile trucks/carts across LA, NYC, and Dallas plus 3 storefronts. — [Foodbeast](https://www.foodbeast.com/news/coolhaus-origin-story-with-natasha-case/); [Gusto](https://gusto.com/resources/articles/business-growth/entrepreneur-advice/coolhaus-growth-strategy)
- **Later trajectory (important caveat):** Coolhaus was later folded into The Urgent Company, a consumer-brands unit of food-tech company Perfect Day. In 2023, Perfect Day laid off ~15% of its workforce and exited the consumer business entirely, shutting down/divesting The Urgent Company (including Coolhaus); a former employee cited that executives were "surprised by retail reset cycles and the length of time it takes to get a new product into stores" — i.e., the CPG/retail scaling path, while initially a success story, ultimately did not sustain as an independently profitable operation under that ownership. The brand was then sold to food-tech firm Superlatus, later acquired by TRxADE Health Inc.; as of 2024 Coolhaus is "mainly available at Whole Foods," and multiple original storefront locations (Culver City, NYC) are now listed as permanently closed on Yelp. — [NOSH, 2023](https://www.nosh.com/news/2023/perfect-day-lays-off-15-of-workforce-shutters-consumer-biz-coolhaus/); [Yelp listings showing closure](https://www.yelp.com/biz/coolhaus-culver-city)
- **Interpretation for this report:** Coolhaus is a genuine success story on "truck to national retail distribution" (strong, well-documented growth, real revenue scale) but a cautionary tale on "retail/CPG scaling sustaining as a standalone profitable business" — the underlying brand survived only by being absorbed/resold, and physical storefronts did not persist.

**The Grilled Cheese Truck / American Patriot Brands (grilled cheese — cautionary/failure case)**
- The original LA truck itself was a genuine single-unit success: reported revenue of $12,000–$15,000 per week and profit margin near 50% in its first year. — [Search synthesis citing cycheese.com analysis](https://cycheese.com/article/why-did-grill-cheese-truck-fail)
- The company pursued franchise expansion and filed Franchise Disclosure Document paperwork in multiple states, announcing "first franchise area development agreement" progress — but according to search-result synthesis, the franchise filings were never fully completed. — [Newswire press releases](https://www.newswire.com/news/the-original-grilled-cheese-truck-executes-first-franchise-area-20089064); [cycheese.com synthesis](https://cycheese.com/article/why-did-grill-cheese-truck-fail)
- The company's corporate entity was later repurposed into American Patriot Brands, which pursued a cannabis-industry pivot and raised more than $30 million from investors; this culminated in an SEC enforcement action resulting in a judgment of nearly $43.9 million in disgorgement, interest, civil penalties, and individual monetary relief against the company, its affiliated entities, and named individuals (Robert Y. Lee and Brian L. Pallas). — [Boof du Jour / SEC case synthesis](https://www.boofdujour.com/post/grilled-cheese-truck-cannabis-sec-judgment)
- **Interpretation for this report:** this is the single clearest documented example in the research of a food-truck concept's growth ambitions (truck → franchise → eventually public-markets vehicle) ending not just in ordinary business failure but in fraud and securities enforcement. It is a strong red-flag case study for governance/capital-raising risk during scaling, distinct from "the food concept itself didn't work."

**Wafels & Dinges (Belgian waffles — NYC truck/cart-to-franchise, directly comparable to a crepes/waffles concept)**
- Founded 2007 by Thomas DeGeest and Rossanna Figuera, starting with a single 1968 Chevy food truck in Manhattan. — [Franchising.com](https://www.franchising.com/news/20220315_nyc_belgian_waffle_institution_wafels__dinges_to_begin_franchising.html)
- Grew over roughly 15 years into multiple trucks, carts, and (increasingly) fixed kiosks in high-traffic NYC locations (e.g., Rockefeller Center), plus outposts reaching Minnesota and Colorado — but did not begin formal franchising until March 2022, i.e., about 15 years after the original truck launched. — [RestaurantNews.com](https://www.restaurantnews.com/iconic-nyc-belgian-waffle-institution-wafels-dinges-to-begin-selective-franchising-nationwide-031122/); [FoodTruckOperator.com](https://www.foodtruckoperator.com/news/wafels-dinges-launches-franchise-strategy/)
- Partnered with DCV Franchise Group as a dedicated strategic franchise-development partner to run the franchising process rather than building that function in-house. — [Franchising.com](https://www.franchising.com/news/20220315_nyc_belgian_waffle_institution_wafels__dinges_to_begin_franchising.html)
- Replicability basis emphasized by the brand: proprietary, hard-to-replicate sourcing (house-made whipped cream, Belgian chocolate import, Argentine dulce de leche import) — i.e., leaning on supply-chain/ingredient sourcing as the "moat," similar in spirit to Cousins' Maine-lobster sourcing story. — [RestaurantNews.com](https://www.restaurantnews.com/iconic-nyc-belgian-waffle-institution-wafels-dinges-to-begin-selective-franchising-nationwide-031122/)
- **Interpretation for this report:** this is a close analog for a crepes/waffles concept — notable because the brand explicitly waited ~15 years and built out multiple non-franchised formats (cart, truck, kiosk) before attempting franchising at all, suggesting a long "prove it out" runway is normal for this food category, not a 2-3 year dash.

**Waffle Love (waffles — Utah truck-to-regional-chain/franchise)**
- Founded by Adam Terry; truck launched July 2012, first brick-and-mortar restaurant opened December 2013 — roughly 18 months truck-to-brick-and-mortar. — [Utah Business](https://www.utahbusiness.com/archive/2015/10/19/utah-food-truck-brings-waffles-with-love/)
- Gained national visibility by placing 2nd on Food Network's "The Great Food Truck Race: All Stars" (2015), which the brand itself and multiple outlets cite as a major growth catalyst. — [Mashed](https://www.mashed.com/427644/the-truth-about-waffle-love-from-great-food-truck-race-all-stars/); [ABC4](https://www.abc4.com/community/the-great-food-truck-race-redemption-and-waffles-how-a-tv-show-helped-heal-utah-brothers-relationship/)
- Over roughly 10+ years, expanded from a single truck to multiple restaurants across 5 states (Utah, Arizona, California, Texas, Idaho); as of the most recent FDD-based data found, the "system" had 16 units since 2015, with investment range of $311,800–$674,700, a $30,000 franchise fee, and a 6% royalty — but almost all locations remained company-owned, with only one or two actual franchised units identified (e.g., Gilbert, AZ; a planned Meridian, ID location). — [Franzy.com FDD analysis](https://franzy.com/franchises/waffle-love); [Idaho Business Review](https://idahobusinessreview.com/2017/09/05/utahs-waffle-love-and-sodalicious-are-expanding-to-treasure-valley/)
- **Interpretation for this report:** Waffle Love shows a brand that nominally "franchises" on paper but has grown almost entirely via company-owned expansion rather than true franchisee-driven replication — a useful caution that "offering franchises" and "successfully franchising" are not the same thing, and that company-owned multi-unit growth (slower, more capital-intensive, more controllable) was the brand's real growth engine.

### Inferences
- Across every successful case (Cousins, Luke's, Wafels & Dinges), a centralized sourcing/processing function for the hero ingredient (lobster, waffle batter/toppings) was established early and is repeatedly cited by the brands themselves as the thing that let them promise consistent quality to a franchisee or new location far from the founder — this is the direct analog to the "obrador"/central-kitchen concept referenced in the brief.
- The time-to-franchise varies enormously by category: Cousins Maine Lobster went from truck to franchising in ~2 years (helped enormously by Shark Tank's national media exposure, which is a non-repeatable advantage most trucks won't have), while Wafels & Dinges wait ~15 years. A premium/complex product with a geographically-anchored sourcing story (lobster) may franchise faster if a strong PR/media moment accelerates demand; a product category without that singular catalyst event may need a decade-plus of proof before franchising.
- Several of the strongest "scaling" stories (Kogi, Coolhaus) actually scaled by diversifying format (brick-and-mortar portfolio; retail/CPG) rather than multiplying the original truck concept directly — worth flagging to the client as a real alternative growth path to "open more trucks" or "franchise the truck."
- Waffle Love's pattern (nominal franchising, real growth via company-owned units) suggests that for a founder who wants control and brand consistency, company-owned multi-unit expansion funded by reinvestment/outside capital may be a more realistic near/medium-term path than true franchising, which requires building out training, FDD compliance, franchisee support infrastructure, and supply-chain contracts that only pay off at larger scale.

### Gaps
- Could not verify a reliable number for how Cousins Maine Lobster's early expansion (2012–2014, pre-franchise) was specifically funded beyond the $55,000 Shark Tank investment — e.g., whether additional outside investors, bank debt, or pure reinvestment covered the gap to the first 13 locations/20 trucks by 2016. The $55K Shark Tank check alone would not cover that buildout, so there is an unsourced funding gap in the public record found here.
- Could not find a specific, named "years from single truck to first franchise agreement" figure for Kogi (it appears Kogi never formally franchised the truck concept, making this question not applicable to that case).
- No reliable public figure found for exactly how much outside capital (if any) Luke's Lobster raised to fund its growth from 1 to 35+ shacks — sources describe the growth and the Cape Seafood vertical integration but not a specific funding breakdown (self-funded vs. investor-funded).

## What capital is typically required to go from one food truck to a second unit, and what financing sources are typically used?

### Takeaway
A second truck typically requires roughly $65,000–$175,000+ in capital depending on new-vs-used and build-out complexity, most commonly financed through SBA 7(a) loans (often capped at $150,000–$250,000 for food-truck-specific collateral due to resale-value limits on the asset) or alternative/equipment financing, with reinvested profit from the first truck as the common "down payment"/equity contribution rather than the sole funding source.

### Cited Findings
- A second, used, fully-equipped-and-permitted truck is cited at roughly $65,000 as a representative figure; more broadly, a new custom-built truck runs $75,000–$200,000 depending on size/equipment/customization, while used trucks/trailers run roughly $20,000–$80,000 but often need refit work that adds to real all-in cost. — [Clarify Capital](https://clarifycapital.com/blog/food-truck-financing); [National Business Capital](https://www.nationalbusinesscapital.com/blog/food-truck-financing/)
- SBA 7(a) loans can lend up to $5 million generally, with terms up to 10 years for working capital and 25 years for real estate — but lenders commonly cap food-truck-specific projects at $150,000–$250,000 because of the truck's comparatively low resale/collateral value versus a building. — [Clarify Capital](https://clarifycapital.com/blog/food-truck-financing); [ProjectionHub SBA guide](https://www.projectionhub.com/post/sba-loan-for-a-food-truck-ultimate-guide)
- Documented real-world example: a barbecue truck owner with ~$380,000 in year-3 revenue, turning down events due to capacity constraints, obtained an SBA 7(a) loan of $120,000 specifically to purchase and equip a second truck. A separate account of a similarly-sized operator ($380,000 revenue) cites a $90,000 term loan used for the same purpose (expanding into a different part of the same city). — [Clarify Capital](https://clarifycapital.com/blog/food-truck-financing); [National Business Capital](https://www.nationalbusinesscapital.com/blog/food-truck-financing/)
- Standard financing-source menu cited across lender/financing sites: SBA 7(a) loans, equipment financing/leasing, term loans, and revenue-based/reinvested profit — with reinvested profit from the first truck typically treated as the necessary proof-of-concept and partial funding base rather than lenders financing 100% of a second unit from a cold start. — [Crestmont Capital financing guide](https://www.crestmontcapital.com/blog/food-truck-business-loans); [GUD Capital](https://gudcapital.com/food-truck-loans/)
- Operator guidance consistently frames the "readiness" signal for a second truck as: the first truck is consistently profitable AND is turning away business due to capacity constraints — i.e., lenders and advisors treat proven, maxed-out unit economics on truck #1 as the real underwriting criterion, more than the applicant's general creditworthiness. — [Clarify Capital](https://clarifycapital.com/blog/food-truck-financing)

### Inferences
- For a premium/high-ACV concept like a lobster roll truck, where a single truck's revenue ceiling is likely higher than a typical truck (Cousins Maine Lobster's ~$1.3M average 2024 per-unit truck sales is a useful benchmark ceiling for "what a well-run premium truck can do"), the $120,000–$175,000 second-truck capital range is a realistic planning figure, with an SBA 7(a) loan plus reinvested profit as the most realistic financing combination — assuming the first truck's financials can be documented to a lender's satisfaction.
- A crepes/waffles concept moving to a bar/lounge format (as implied by "truck/bar" in the brief) likely faces a materially different, larger capital ask than a second truck, closer to brick-and-mortar buildout costs (see Cousins' $267,000–$969,000 storefront investment range as a rough analog for what a fixed "bar" format might require) rather than the $65K–$175K truck-to-truck figure.

### Gaps
- No source found gives a lobster-specific or crepe/waffle-specific second-unit capital figure (e.g., a specialized refrigeration/live-tank buildout cost for a second lobster truck, or a waffle-bar buildout cost) — the figures above are generic food-truck financing benchmarks, not category-specific, and should be treated as a planning range rather than a precise estimate for either Miami concept.
- Could not find reliable data on what share of second-truck purchases are financed via SBA loans vs. pure cash reinvestment vs. outside investors — the search results provide illustrative anecdotes (2 specific loan examples) rather than an industry-wide breakdown.

## What role does a commissary/shared-kitchen or central-prep-kitchen model play in food truck concepts that successfully scaled, versus those that stayed single-unit or failed?

### Takeaway
Every successfully-scaled case study in this research built or required a centralized prep/processing function (an "obrador" analog) specifically to standardize the hero product across distant units — this is treated by both the brands themselves and by franchise-development commentary as a precondition for scaling, not an optional efficiency play; commissary/ghost-kitchen infrastructure can also independently enable faster (30–60 day) launch of new units.

### Cited Findings
- Cousins Maine Lobster's national scaling model centers on a planned, centralized lobster supply chain (volume planned years/quarters/months/weeks ahead) feeding franchisee-run local commissaries, with company-mandated designated suppliers for food, beverage, equipment and fixtures to protect consistency. — [Food Truck Operator](https://www.foodtruckoperator.com/articles/cousins-maine-lobster-keeps-growth-churning-through-authenticity/)
- Luke's Lobster explicitly built its own seafood processing facility (Cape Seafood, Saco, Maine) in 2012 as it scaled past its first couple of shacks — vertical integration of the commissary function rather than leaving each shack to source/prep independently. — [QSR Web](https://www.qsrweb.com/blogs/lukes-lobster-surpasses-10-year-milestone-delivering-sustainable-seafood/)
- General commissary-kitchen industry commentary: commissary kitchens let food businesses (including food trucks) scale production up or down without committing to fixed infrastructure, and are described as a "plug-and-play" solution for scaling without the cost of a full-scale facility; ghost-kitchen/commissary setups can enable a new unit to launch in as little as 30–60 days. — [ModularCulinaire](https://www.modularculinaire.com/blog/commissary-kitchen); [BistroBuddy](https://bistrobuddy.com/news/local-ghost-kitchen-news/commissary-kitchens-a-complete-guide-for-food-entrepreneurs)
- Franchise-development commentary frames supply-chain/commissary infrastructure as a prerequisite, not a byproduct, of franchising: "A food truck concept built around quality — whether fresh ingredients or proprietary recipes — needs supply chain infrastructure before it can grow," and franchisors are advised to secure reliable regional/national distributors "before" scaling, with documented recipes/prep procedures that explicitly account for how the concept runs within a truck's limited space. — [FranchiseCreator.com](https://franchisecreator.com/franchise-food-truck-business/); [FranchiseGenesis](https://franchisegenesis.com/food-truck-franchising/)
- Conversely, Wafels & Dinges' sourcing story (importing Belgian chocolate, making its own whipped cream, importing Argentine dulce de leche) is explicitly positioned by the brand as its hard-to-copy "moat" underpinning its 2022 franchising push — again, a centralized/standardized-sourcing argument preceding franchising, not a side effect of it. — [RestaurantNews.com](https://www.restaurantnews.com/iconic-nyc-belgian-waffle-institution-wafels-dinges-to-begin-selective-franchising-nationwide-031122/)

### Inferences
- For a premium lobster roll truck, the Cousins/Luke's pattern strongly suggests that before (or concurrent with) adding a second unit, the Miami operator should lock down a reliable, cost-predictable live-lobster/lobster-meat supply chain and ideally a dedicated prep space separate from the truck itself — without that, each new unit re-exposes the business to commodity price volatility and inconsistent product quality that undermines a "premium" positioning.
- For the crepes/waffles truck/bar, the Wafels & Dinges pattern suggests the equivalent move is standardizing and centrally sourcing the few ingredients that define product quality (batter formulation, specific chocolate/dulce de leche/cream sourcing) and documenting exact prep specs before attempting a second location — the ingredient-sourcing "story" can double as marketing and as the operational backbone for replication.
- The 30–60 day ghost-kitchen/commissary launch timeline figure suggests that for a Miami-based second unit (if it is to be a brick-and-mortar/bar extension rather than a second truck), using an existing third-party commissary or shared kitchen initially — rather than building a dedicated central kitchen immediately — could meaningfully de-risk and speed up a first expansion attempt.

### Gaps
- No case study found explicitly documents a food truck that "stayed single-unit or failed specifically because it lacked a commissary model" — the research surfaced strong positive evidence (successful scalers built commissary/central-sourcing functions) but no clean negative case directly attributing failure-to-scale to absence of a commissary, as opposed to other factors (capital, demand, management). This causal claim should be treated as a reasonable inference from the pattern, not a separately sourced fact.

## Red flags and success factors associated with food-truck-to-multi-unit transitions

### Takeaway
The clearest, most consistently cited success factors are: (1) documented, teachable recipes/processes that do not depend on the founder's personal presence or relationships; (2) proven, maxed-out unit economics on the first unit before committing capital to a second; (3) a locked-down supply chain for the hero ingredient; and (4) realistic, incremental growth pacing. The clearest red flags are: founder-dependent operations (relationships, improvisation, personal cooking skill) that don't transfer; premature/aggressive franchise or capital-raise activity before the concept is proven (the Grilled Cheese Truck/American Patriot Brands case); and quality-control dilution from growing too fast across dispersed, low-visibility mobile units.

### Cited Findings
- "If your food truck's success depends on your personal presence — relationships with event organizers, on-the-fly decisions — franchising will expose that dependency immediately, so building systems that make the operation work without you is essential," and "every recipe—every dish, sauce, and signature item—must be documented with precise measurements, defined preparation steps, temperatures, and timing... precisely enough that a franchisee with no connection to your original operation can replicate it." — [FasterCapital food-truck-franchise synthesis](https://fastercapital.com/content/Food-Truck-Franchise--How-to-Build-a-Successful-Food-Truck-Franchise-from-Scratch.html); [FMS Franchise](https://www.fmsfranchise.com/how-to-franchise-a-food-truck-business-a-recipe-for-success/)
- "Franchises with inconsistent quality control lose up to 37% of customers after just one bad brand experience," and roughly 20% of food franchises reportedly struggle with standardization/quality-control issues during their first three years of expansion. — [FieldPie](https://www.fieldpie.com/blog/franchise-quality-control/); [industry synthesis via search](https://www.gilkeyrestaurantconsulting.com/news/how-multi-unit-restaurants-maintain-quality-across-locations)
- "Rushing to open too many franchises too quickly can dilute the brand and compromise quality... weak training is one of the most common causes of franchisee failure." — [Franchising.com](https://www.franchising.com/articles/20250106_how_do_you_ensure_that_each_of_your_units_maintains_brand_consistency_w.html)
- A dispersed fleet of trucks (vs. fixed-address restaurants) makes consistency monitoring both more necessary and more operationally difficult, requiring franchisors to actively track each unit's metrics via inspections and customer feedback. — [Reide Law Firm / franchise-mobile-food synthesis](https://reidellawfirm.com/franchising-mobile-food-trucks/)
- Mobile operations add specific complexity to recipe documentation: "limited space, on-board equipment, and prep timing" mean standard restaurant SOPs don't directly transfer and must be rewritten for how the concept actually runs on a truck. — [FranchiseCreator.com](https://franchisecreator.com/franchise-food-truck-business/)
- The Grilled Cheese Truck case (above) stands as the starkest documented red-flag example: an initially profitable single truck pursued aggressive franchise-paperwork activity and ultimately capital-raising (as American Patriot Brands) well beyond what its core food concept could support, ending in an SEC fraud judgment of ~$43.9 million — illustrating how capital-raising ambition can become disconnected from (and ultimately unrelated to) the underlying food business's actual proven unit economics. — [Boof du Jour synthesis](https://www.boofdujour.com/post/grilled-cheese-truck-cannabis-sec-judgment)
- General food-truck market commentary (2025) notes rising inflation in ingredients and transportation costs, permitting/logistics burdens, and maintenance costs as current headwinds that squeeze margins even for established operators — a background risk factor for any expansion decision right now, not specific to failed scalers but relevant to underwriting any 2025-2026 expansion plan. — [CNBC, Nov 2025](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/11/01/food-truck-restaurant-billion-dollar-business-keys-success-costs.html)

### Inferences
- For both Miami concepts, the practical translation of "founder dependency" risk is: can the lobster-roll preparation and the crepe/waffle preparation currently be executed to the same quality standard by a trained employee with no founder involvement? If not, that gap should be treated as the single highest-priority item to close before any second-unit investment, since it is the factor cited most consistently across both positive (Cousins, Luke's, Wafels & Dinges) and negative (Grilled Cheese Truck implicitly) cases.
- Given the quality-control dilution risk cited for dispersed mobile units specifically (vs. fixed restaurants), a second truck (same format, same inherent monitoring difficulty) may actually carry more consistency risk per unit than a first brick-and-mortar/bar expansion of the same concept, where quality can be more directly supervised — worth weighing explicitly against the lower capital cost of a second truck.

### Gaps
- Could not find a specific, named industry statistic quantifying "founder-dependency" as a measured cause of food-truck-to-multi-unit failure (e.g., "% of failed food truck expansions attributed to founder dependency") — the finding above is well-supported qualitatively across multiple franchise-development sources but not expressed anywhere found as a hard percentage.
- No additional post-mortem case study of a food truck chain failing specifically from premature national ambition (beyond the Grilled Cheese Truck/American Patriot Brands case) was identified in the time available; this key question is answered with one strong example rather than a broader pattern of named failures.
